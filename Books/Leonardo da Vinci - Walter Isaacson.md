# Leonardo da Vinci — Walter Isaacson (2017)

Authorized biography of Renaissance polymath Leonardo da Vinci. Isaacson spent years studying Leonardo's 7,200+ surviving notebook pages (from an estimated 13,000+ originally produced). Based on primary source analysis of the notebooks — spanning anatomy, flying machines, hydraulics, optics, geology, botany, military engineering, and painting — plus scholarship from art historians, historians of science, and museum curators worldwide. Published October 17, 2017. 600+ pages. Isaacson's thesis: Leonardo's genius was not God-given but self-cultivated — a learnable model of curiosity, observation, and cross-disciplinary imagination.

## Life Arc

### Early Life (1452–1469)
- Born April 15, 1452 in Vinci, Tuscany — illegitimate son of Ser Piero da Vinci (notary) and Caterina (peasant woman); surname "da Vinci" means simply "from Vinci," not a family name
- Because he was illegitimate, he was excluded from Latin schools and formal guild apprenticeship tracks — paradoxically freeing him from rote classical education; he never deferred to Aristotle or Galen when his eyes told him otherwise
- Raised partly by uncle Francesco in the countryside; absorbed a deep love of nature, birds, water, and light from childhood observation
- No university, no Latin, no formal theology — "unlettered man" (uomo senza lettere) was an insult he wore as a badge and reversed into pride
- Self-taught in mathematics, Latin, and geometry as an adult

### Apprenticeship Under Verrocchio (1466–1476)
- At ~14, apprenticed to **Andrea del Verrocchio** in Florence — the city's premier workshop (bottega); trained alongside Botticelli
- Verrocchio's bottega produced sculpture, painting, goldsmithing, stage design, and military engineering — a cross-disciplinary environment that shaped Leonardo permanently
- **Baptism of Christ (c.1475)** — Leonardo's angel in the lower left stunned Verrocchio; Vasari claimed Verrocchio vowed never to paint again after seeing it; whether true or not, Verrocchio handed Leonardo increasingly important commissions
- 1476: Accused of sodomy with a young model (Jacopo Saltarelli); charges were filed anonymously, twice, then dropped; likely consensual and widely known in Florence; Isaacson treats Leonardo's homosexuality as a settled biographical fact

### First Milan Period (1482–1499)
- Left Florence for Milan; wrote a remarkable letter to **Ludovico Sforza** (Duke of Milan) offering his services primarily as a military engineer — bridges, cannons, armored vehicles, siege machines — with painting mentioned last as almost an afterthought; reveals his self-image was engineer first, artist second
- **The Virgin of the Rocks (1483–1486)** — first major Milan commission; sfumato technique fully developed; revolutionary atmospheric depth
- **Lady with an Ermine (c.1489–1490)** — Cecilia Gallerani, Ludovico's mistress; the most psychologically alive portrait since antiquity; subject appears to react to something outside the frame (a then-unprecedented compositional device)
- Collaborated with mathematician **Luca Pacioli** (*De Divina Proportione*); drew the illustrations; exposed to Euclidean geometry formally for the first time as an adult
- **The Last Supper (1495–1498)** — painted on refectory wall of Santa Maria delle Grazie; Isaacson's most detailed chapter; captures the instant after Christ says "one of you will betray me"; each apostle's face and gesture is a distinct psychological study; painted in experimental tempera on dry plaster (not true fresco) — began deteriorating within decades but remains the most analyzed painting in history
- Notebooks from this period: flying machine designs (ornithopter, hang glider, aerial screw/proto-helicopter), hydraulic machinery, anatomical studies, geometry puzzles, shopping lists, to-do items ("describe the tongue of a woodpecker")

### Wandering Period (1499–1513)
- French invaded Milan (1499); Leonardo fled to Venice (briefly), then returned to Florence
- **Cesare Borgia phase (1502–1503)**: hired as military engineer and cartographer for Borgia's campaigns across central Italy; produced some of the most accurate maps of his era, including a bird's-eye map of Imola; traveled with Niccolò Machiavelli (Borgia's political analyst) — the two almost certainly met
- **Battle of Anghiari (1503–1506)**: massive mural commissioned for the Great Council Hall of Florence; Leonardo experimented with encaustic technique; the mural was abandoned unfinished after the wax medium ran; survives only in copies; Isaacson treats the unfinished status as emblematic of Leonardo's lifelong pattern
- **Mona Lisa (c.1503–1517)**: began in Florence, carried everywhere, worked on intermittently for ~14 years, never delivered to the client; the sfumato technique, the missing eyebrows, the atmospheric perspective, the sitter's identity (Lisa Gherardini, wife of Francesco del Giocondo), the mystery of the smile — Isaacson devotes three chapters to it

### Rome and Final Years (1513–1519)
- Moved to Rome under patronage of **Giuliano de' Medici** (Pope Leo X's brother); relatively unproductive — Vatican intrigues, a spy among his assistants, disputes over a dissection permit, rivalry with Michelangelo and Raphael
- Continued anatomical dissections; produced extraordinarily accurate drawings of the heart, valves, and blood flow — 150+ years ahead of Harvey's published discovery of circulation
- **Gian Giacomo Caprotti ("Salai")**: mischievous assistant and almost certainly romantic partner; had been with Leonardo since age 10 (1490); Leonardo called him "little devil"; left him half the Milan vineyard in his will
- **Francesco Melzi**: nobleman's son, devoted pupil and final companion from ~1508; inherited Leonardo's notebooks and attempted (incompletely) to preserve them
- Invited to France by **King Francis I**; settled at **Château du Clos Lucé** near Amboise; given the title "First Painter, Engineer, and Architect to the King"; the king reportedly wept at his deathbed
- Died May 2, 1519, age 67; left 17–20 surviving paintings (out of ~30 begun) and ~7,200 pages of notebooks

## Key Themes

### 1. Insatiable, Undisciplined Curiosity
- The defining feature of Leonardo's character; his notebooks begin with questions — "Why is the sky blue?" "How does a woodpecker's tongue work?" "What does the inside of a skull look like?" — across every domain simultaneously
- Isaacson argues this was self-cultivated, not innate; Leonardo trained himself to notice what others walked past
- The curiosity had no boundaries and no hierarchy of value; a trick of light on a soap bubble was as worthy of investigation as the anatomy of the heart
- "Learning never exhausts the mind" — the productive capacity of curiosity, not its output, was his treasure

### 2. Observation Over Authority
- Leonardo refused to defer to Aristotle or Galen when his eyes contradicted them — radical for the 15 th century
- "If you find from your own experience that something is a fact and it contradicts what some authority has written down, then you must abandon the authority and base your reasoning on your own findings."
- Dissected 30+ human corpses; produced anatomical drawings so accurate that Harvey's 1628 discovery of blood circulation was visible in Leonardo's 1510 notebooks (unpublished)
- The optic nerve, the aortic valve function, the curvature of the spine — all corrected versus classical authority

### 3. Cross-Disciplinary Synthesis as the Source of Innovation
- Leonardo had no word for "artist" versus "scientist" — to him, understanding nature and depicting it were the same inquiry
- His painting required optics (how the eye perceives), geology (what the rocks look like), botany (accurate plant rendering), anatomy (muscles beneath the skin), and atmospheric physics (why distant things look blue/hazy)
- His engineering required the same visual imagination he used for painting
- Isaacson's primary lesson: the innovators who matter most are usually those who cross domain lines

### 4. The Notebooks as Thinking Tool
- ~7,200 surviving pages; notebooks were his cognitive workspace, not a diary of completed thoughts
- Contents: shopping lists alongside flying machine designs; anatomical drawings next to geometry proofs; water flow studies next to recipes; architectural designs beside doodles of faces
- He thought by writing and drawing simultaneously; the image and the word were a single cognitive act
- Never published; the notebooks scattered after his death, passed through dozens of hands over centuries, ended up in libraries in Milan (Codex Atlanticus, ~1,119 pages), Windsor, the Vatican, Madrid, Paris, Seattle (Gates bought the Codex Leicester for $30.8 M in 1994)

### 5. Sfumato — Blurred Edges as Artistic and Intellectual Style
- Italian for "vanished into smoke"; Leonardo's technique of eliminating harsh outlines in painting, replacing them with gradual tonal gradations
- Technically: built up 30+ ultra-thin glazes of paint; applied with his fingertips (confirmed by fingerprint analysis)
- Philosophically: reflected his scientific understanding that edges in nature are never sharp — that reality is continuous, not discrete
- Isaacson extends sfumato metaphorically: Leonardo's comfort with ambiguity and unanswered questions was a cognitive virtue, not a weakness — "the willingness to embrace uncertainty"

### 6. The Procrastination Problem
- Leonardo left ~10 major works unfinished; clients sued him, wrote pleading letters, gave up; the Adoration of the Magi (1481) was abandoned when he left for Milan and never returned to it; the Battle of Anghiari was abandoned mid-painting
- Isaacson's interpretation: not laziness but the compulsive pull of new curiosities; once he understood a problem well enough, solving it into a finished product was less interesting than the next question
- Also perfectionism — the Mona Lisa was carried everywhere for ~14 years because it was never quite right
- The unfinished works are not failures; they are evidence of the cost of unlimited curiosity

### 7. Anatomy — The Scientific Feat Hidden in Plain Sight
- Obtained permission to dissect corpses at hospitals in Florence and Milan; worked in cold rooms to slow decomposition
- Drew the heart valves and described their function correctly (the helical flow of blood through the aortic valve); this wasn't confirmed experimentally until the 1980 s using CT scans
- Drew the fetus in utero, the layers of abdominal muscles, the shoulder and arm musculature in mechanical diagrams showing how muscles function
- Never published any of it; had Melzi compiled and published it, it would have been the greatest scientific publication of the Renaissance

### 8. Flying — The Lifelong Obsession
- Filled more notebook pages with the study of bird flight than almost any other single topic
- Designed the ornithopter (human-powered flapping wing), the aerial screw (proto-helicopter), the hang glider, and a parachute
- Watched birds for thousands of hours; mapped the physics of lift, drag, and glide angle with astonishing accuracy for someone without calculus
- Never built a working flying machine; the Isaacson thesis: his inability to finish didn't diminish the intellectual achievement

## Isaacson's 20 Lessons from Leonardo

1. **Be curious, relentlessly** — about everything, not just your specialty
2. **Seek knowledge for its own sake** — not to be practical, not for status
3. **Retain a child's sense of wonder** — the mature adult's curse is learning to stop noticing
4. **Observe carefully** — really look; most people see without perceiving
5. **Start with the details** — then pull back; data before theory
6. **See things unseen** — notice the curl of a lip before it becomes a full smile; that's what goes in the painting
7. **Go down rabbit holes** — the woodpecker's tongue led to a whole theory of animal mechanics
8. **Get distracted, deliberately** — interdisciplinary wandering is where synthesis lives
9. **Respect facts, not authority** — your eyes beat any text written 1,000 years ago
10. **Think visually** — draw what you're thinking; a picture is a hypothesis
11. **Avoid disciplinary silos** — never call yourself just a painter or just an engineer
12. **Let the perfect fight the good** — sometimes; great works need time
13. **But also finish things** — unfinished notebooks benefited no one
14. **Collaborate** — the bottega model; other minds find what yours misses
15. **Take notes on paper** — the act of drawing a thing is the act of understanding it
16. **Be open to mystery** — sfumato applies to knowledge too; not every question needs a definitive answer
17. **Indulge fantasy** — his flying machines were "unrealistic" in 1490; they were not wrong
18. **Study science to make better art** — and study art to be a better scientist
19. **Approach obstacles as engineering problems** — how does it actually work?
20. **The intersection of art and science is where the magic lives** — this is Isaacson's explicit through-line for the entire trilogy (Leonardo → Einstein → Jobs)

## Critical Reception
- **Commercially**: immediate bestseller; millions of copies sold worldwide; one of the most successful biographies of the 2010 s
- **Praise**: widely praised for accessibility and for making Leonardo legible to a modern audience; scientists and engineers appreciated the equal weight given to Leonardo's technical notebooks
- **Criticism**: art historians complained Isaacson overweighted the "lessons for moderns" framing at the expense of rigorous historical contextualization; some felt the book romanticized Leonardo's procrastination rather than critiquing it
- **Uniqueness**: the only major popular biography of Leonardo written with sustained attention to the notebooks as primary source, not just the paintings
