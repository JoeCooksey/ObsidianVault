# Lally 2009 — How Are Habits Formed

University College London study (Lally et al., *European Journal of Social Psychology*, 2010 — widely dated to its 2009 acceptance). Tracked **96 participants over 12 weeks** as each chose one new daily health behavior (e.g., drinking water after breakfast, a walk before dinner) tied to a consistent cue, and self-rated automaticity each day.

## Key Findings

- **Median time to automaticity: ~66 days.** This is the empirical replacement for the folk "21 days" figure (which traces to Maxwell Maltz's 1960 *Psycho-Cybernetics* observation about patients, not a habit study).
- **Range: 18 to 254 days.** Simple behaviors (drinking water) automated fast; complex ones (50 sit-ups) took far longer. *How automatic a daily learning block becomes depends on how small and well-cued it is.*
- **Automaticity rises along an asymptotic curve** — fast early gains, then plateau. Repetition matters most at the start.
- **Missing one day did not break the curve.** A single lapse "did not materially affect the habit formation process." This is the empirical basis for the [[Never Miss Twice]] rule.

## What It Contributes

This is the primary-source anchor for the *timeline and forgiveness* of building a daily learning habit: expect ~2 months of deliberate repetition before a learning block runs on autopilot, keep the behavior small to automate it faster, and treat a single missed day as noise, not failure.

> [!note] The vault already holds Huberman's [[21-Day Habit Formation System]] and [[Wendy Wood]]'s habit work. Lally is the complementary primary source — the 21-day figure is better read as "a period to attempt deliberate change," not a deadline for automaticity.
