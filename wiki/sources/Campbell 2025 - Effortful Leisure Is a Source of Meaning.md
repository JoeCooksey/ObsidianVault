# Campbell et al. 2025 — Effortful Leisure Is a Source of Meaning in Everyday Life

Peer-reviewed paper, *Communications Psychology* (Nature Portfolio), 2025. Five studies, **N = 2,569**, testing whether effort in leisure trades against or contributes to wellbeing.

## What it contributes

This is the strongest recent evidence for *why* the high-effort options in [[Free Time Tier List]] beat the passive defaults: effort is the thing that turns a free hour into a *meaningful* one. It also names the bias to beat — people consistently pick the easy option even though it leaves them emptier.

## Design

- **Study 1**: 1,145 undergraduates rate daily activities on effort and meaning.
- **Studies 2a–2b** (N=375, N=389): Sudoku puzzles vs. watching video.
- **Study 3** (N=400): Sudoku vs. a low-effort click-to-reveal task.
- **Study 4** (N=260): experience sampling over 8 days (real life, not lab).

## Key findings

- **Effortful leisure is rated more meaningful** than passive leisure, consistently. (confidence: high)
- The lab "trade-off" (Study 1: effortful = *more meaningful but less enjoyable*) **did not hold in real life**: in Study 4, within-person effort predicted meaning (b = 0.26, p < .001) but **did not predict lower leisure enjoyment**. Effort bought meaning essentially for free. (confidence: high)
- Difficulty has diminishing returns: medium and hard Sudoku scored equally on meaning (M = 3.63 each), both far above video (M = 2.38) — you need *some* challenge, not maximal challenge. (confidence: high)
- **People under-select effortful leisure**: "low-effort activities dominate people's free time," so most people routinely miss the meaning available to them. (confidence: high)
- Examples used — effortful: Sudoku, recreational sports, board games, volunteering. Passive: watching YouTube/TV. (confidence: high)

## Limits

Sudoku-vs-video is a clean but narrow operationalization of "effort." Undergraduate-heavy samples. The meaning measure is self-reported. Robust direction, modest effect sizes.
