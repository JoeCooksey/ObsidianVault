# Organic Nutrition Evidence (Stanford 2012 + Newcastle 2014)

The two landmark meta-analyses on organic vs. conventional food. They reach **different conclusions on nutrition** but **agree on pesticides** — the cleanest way to read the evidence.

## Stanford 2012 (Smith-Spangler et al., *Annals of Internal Medicine*)
- Reviewed **237 studies** (17 human, 223 nutrient/contamination comparisons).
- **Found little evidence** that organic foods are more nutritious or that eating organic improves health.
- Did find organic produce is **~30% less likely** to be contaminated with pesticides.
- Conclusion popularized as: "organic isn't healthier."

## Newcastle 2014 (Barański et al., *British Journal of Nutrition*)
- Meta-analysis of **343 peer-reviewed publications**.
- Organic crops had **statistically significantly higher antioxidants/polyphenols** (range cited 18–69% for some compound classes).
- **Lower cadmium** (a toxic heavy metal) and **lower pesticide-residue incidence** (~4× less likely to carry residues).
- Conclusion popularized as: "organic has more good stuff, less bad stuff."

## How To Reconcile Them (confidence: high on the points of agreement)
- **Both agree** organic = meaningfully lower pesticide and (per Newcastle) heavy-metal load.
- They disagree on **antioxidant magnitude and whether it matters clinically** — methodology and which compounds were pooled differ.
- **Neither** found a long-term study showing organic eaters are actually *healthier* — the outcome data doesn't exist yet. Three systematic reviews (2010, 2012, 2019) of health outcomes showed no clear benefit, limited by heterogeneity and bias.

## Bottom Line
The defensible claim is **reduced exposure** (pesticides, cadmium), **not** "more nutritious" or "makes you healthier." That reframes organic as a *risk-reduction* purchase, which is exactly why the [[EWG Shopper's Guide to Pesticides in Produce (2025)|Dirty Dozen]] targeting works: spend on the high-residue items, skip the premium on low-residue ones.
