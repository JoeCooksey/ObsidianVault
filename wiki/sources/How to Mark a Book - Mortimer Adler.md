# How to Mark a Book — Mortimer Adler (1940 essay; precursor to *How to Read a Book*, 1940/1972)

Adler's short, famous essay arguing that **active reading requires marking the book**. It is the foundational source for the *during-reading* (marginalia) layer of book note-taking.

## The Core Argument

> *"Marking up a book is not an act of mutilation but of love."* (attributed, Adler)

- Reading must be **active** to accomplish anything more than passing time.
- Writing by hand **"brings words and sentences more sharply before your mind and preserves them better in your memory."** (Source: search summaries of the essay)
- Marking forces you to **stay awake** (genuinely conscious), to think, and to **express your reactions in words** — which sharpens questions and fixes understanding.

## Three Kinds of Book Owners

1. Those with a few books, untouched and clean.
2. Those with many books, most unread, all pristine.
3. Those with **few or many books, every one marked and dog-eared** — broken in from cover to cover. **Only the third truly owns their books.**

## The Marking Toolkit

| Device | Used for |
|--------|----------|
| **Underlining** | Major points; forceful or important statements |
| **Vertical line in the margin** | Emphasize a statement already underlined, or mark a passage too long to underline |
| **Star / asterisk in the margin** | The most important statements — used *sparingly* (e.g. ~10–12 per book) |
| **Numbers in the margin** | The sequence of points in an argument |
| **Numbers of other pages** | Cross-references — where the author makes the same or a contradictory point ("Cf. p. 62") |
| **Circling key words or phrases** | Same function as underlining, for terms |
| **Writing in the margins / top & bottom / endpapers** | Questions, your answers, reducing a complex passage to a simple statement, recording the structure of the book |

The blank endpapers at the back are for building **your own index of the author's points in your own words** as you go.
