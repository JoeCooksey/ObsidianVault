# Suez 2022 — Sweeteners Alter Human Glucose Tolerance (Cell)

The strongest *human* evidence that sweeteners are not metabolically inert. A **randomized controlled trial of 120 healthy adults** given saccharin, sucralose, aspartame, or stevia sachets for 2 weeks at **doses below the acceptable daily intake**, vs vehicle-glucose and no-supplement controls. From the Weizmann Institute (follow-up to the 2014 Nature mouse+pilot study).

## Key Findings (According to PubMed)
- **Each sweetener distinctly altered the stool and oral microbiome and the plasma metabolome.**
- **Saccharin and sucralose significantly impaired glycemic responses** (glucose tolerance) — aspartame and stevia did not, in this trial.
- **Causality demonstrated**: germ-free mice colonized with stool from the top vs bottom human glycemic responders reproduced the donors' glycemic responses → the effect is transmitted *by the microbiome*, not the chemical directly.
- Responses are **person-specific** — the same sweetener helps some, harms others, depending on baseline microbiome. See [[Personalized Glycemic Response to Sweeteners]].

## What It Means
- Overturns the "inert, passes through unchanged" assumption for saccharin/sucralose.
- BUT: 2-week surrogate endpoint (glycemic response), small n, healthy adults, no hard clinical outcomes. Doesn't prove diet soda causes diabetes; proves a plausible *mechanism* and that effects are heterogeneous.
- Reconciles part of the RCT-vs-cohort puzzle: if harm is real but person-specific, population averages wash it out while susceptible subgroups drive cohort signals.
