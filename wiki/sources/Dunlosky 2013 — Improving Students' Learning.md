# Dunlosky 2013 — Improving Students' Learning

**Full title:** *"Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques: Promising Directions From Cognitive and Educational Psychology."* Published in *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, vol. 14, pp. 4–58 (2013). The **empirical backbone** of the modern science of learning.

## What it did

Reviewed and graded **10 commonly used learning techniques** against the experimental evidence, rating each as **high, medium, or low utility** by how broadly it works (across ages, abilities, materials, and test types). A later confirmatory meta-analysis (Frontiers, 2021) of 242 studies / 169,179 participants found an overall mean effect size of **0.56**.

## The rankings

| Utility | Technique |
|---|---|
| **High** | **Practice testing** (retrieval), **Distributed practice** (spacing) |
| **Moderate** | Elaborative interrogation, Self-explanation, **Interleaved practice** |
| **Low** | Summarization, **Highlighting/underlining**, Keyword mnemonic, Imagery for text, **Rereading** |

## Why it matters

- The two winners — **[[Active Recall (Retrieval Practice)|practice testing]]** and **[[Spaced Repetition|distributed practice]]** — are *low-effort, free, and broadly applicable*, yet rarely taught.
- The two most *popular* student strategies — rereading and highlighting — are rated **low utility**. People spend their study time on the worst-performing methods.
- Effects are **much larger with feedback** and for **lower-ability learners** — but help nearly everyone.

> [!note] Caveat
> Most studies measured surface/factual outcomes; evidence for *far transfer* to complex skills is thinner. The techniques are proven for *retention*; understanding still needs tools like [[The Feynman Technique]].

This source grounds the claim that the [[Research: How to Learn Anything (The Science of Learning)|core study stack]] should be retrieval + spacing + interleaving.
