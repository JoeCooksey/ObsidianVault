# Klingberg & Sauce 2022 — Gaming and Children's IQ

**Published:** *Scientific Reports* (Nature), 12 May 2022. Authors: Bruno Sauce, Magnus Liebherr, Nicholas Judd, Torkel Klingberg (Karolinska Institutet + Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam).

The single strongest causal-leaning study linking video games to measured intelligence gains.

## Design
- Used data from the **Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD)** project.
- **~9,000 US children** assessed at age 9–10; **5,000+ followed up two years later** (age 11–12).
- Intelligence measured by five tasks: reading comprehension + vocabulary, attention/executive function, visual-spatial processing, and multi-trial learning. Combined into a single intelligence score.
- Screen time split into **gaming, watching videos/TV, and social media**.
- **First study of its kind to control for genetic effects** (polygenic scores) alongside socioeconomic background (household income, parental education, neighborhood quality).

## Key Findings
- Children in the **top ~17% of gaming hours increased their intelligence ~2.5 IQ points more than the average child** over the two years. (confidence: high — large sample, genetics + SES controlled)
- **Watching videos/TV and social media had neither a positive nor negative effect** on the intelligence change. Gaming was the only screen activity associated with a gain.
- Effect held after controlling for genetics and SES, strengthening (but not proving) a causal interpretation.

## Caveats Noted by Authors
- Not a randomized trial — residual confounding is possible.
- Did **not** measure mental health, sleep quality, or physical exercise, which gaming time can displace.
- Authors explicitly say this is **"not a blanket recommendation for limitless gaming."**
- Did not distinguish game genres — "gaming" was aggregate hours, so it cannot rank specific games.

## Contribution to Topic
This is the headline evidence that gaming *as a category* tracks real fluid-intelligence gains in developing brains — but because it lumps all games together, it cannot build a tier list by genre. It establishes the ceiling claim ("yes, gaming can raise measured IQ") that genre-specific studies then refine.
