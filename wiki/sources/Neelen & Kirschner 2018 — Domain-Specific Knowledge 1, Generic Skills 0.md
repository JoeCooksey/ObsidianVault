# Neelen & Kirschner 2018 — Domain-Specific Knowledge 1, Generic Skills 0

Blog essay by Mirjam Neelen and **Paul A. Kirschner** (prominent educational psychologist, cognitive-load tradition). Polemical but grounded in mainstream cognitive science; treated as a strong synthesis of an established position, hence **medium** confidence (opinion piece restating peer-reviewed findings).

## The argument

There is no general, content-free "problem-solving skill" you can train in the abstract and deploy anywhere. Competence in a domain depends overwhelmingly on **domain-specific knowledge stored in long-term memory**.

## Evidence cited

- **De Groot (1946) chess studies** — masters beat novices because they *recognize board patterns*, not via superior general reasoning.
- **Chi, Feltovich & Glaser (1979, 1982)** — experts use organized schemas; novices fall back on weak general methods like **means–end analysis**.
- **Tricot & Sweller** — domain-specific knowledge is "arguably the most important factor, and possibly the only factor, determining acquired cognitive performance."

## Mechanism

Working memory handles familiar (schema-backed) information almost without limit but chokes on novel information. Without relevant prior knowledge, the solver is reduced to trial-and-error.

## Caveat / contrast

This is the strong domain-specific position. It usefully corrects "teach generic critical thinking" hype, but other work shows **transferable process** (Pólya heuristics, metacognition, analogical comparison) *does* help — provided the learner already has domain knowledge to apply it to.
