# Tamborini, Kim & Sakamoto 2015 — Education and Lifetime Earnings

Peer-reviewed study in *Demography* analyzing Survey of Income and Program Participation (SIPP) data linked to Social Security Administration tax records, 1982–2008. Measures **50-year lifetime earnings** (ages 20–69) by education level — the gold-standard answer to "what is the financial return on schooling."

## What it contributes

This is the **hard-evidence backbone** for the financial case that investing in your own human capital pays. Unlike opinion pieces, it uses linked administrative tax records and corrects a major methodological error in older estimates.

- **Gross lifetime earnings (men):** HS diploma $1.54M → bachelor's $2.43M (gap ~$896K) → graduate degree $3.05M.
- **Gross (women):** HS $811K → bachelor's $1.44M (gap ~$630K) → graduate $1.87M.
- **Net of covariates** (demographics, academic preparation), the HS→BA gap shrinks ~30%: **~$655K (men), ~$445K (women)**.
- **Net present value at age 20** (4% discount): bachelor's advantage **~$259K (men), ~$180K (women)**.
- The regression-adjusted BA premium is **~43% higher earnings for men, ~51% for women** vs HS grads.

> [!warning] Caveat
> The authors show that the widely-cited "synthetic cohort" method (extrapolating from a single year's cross-section) **overstates lifetime returns by 23% for men and 42% for women**. Treat the bigger numbers you see quoted elsewhere with suspicion.

A companion causal study ([Heckman-style structural model](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6190599/)) finds **selection bias accounts for over half** of observed wage gaps, and that college returns are **concentrated among higher-ability individuals** — "graduating college is not a wise choice for all." The return is real but conditional, not automatic.
