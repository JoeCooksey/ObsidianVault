# Cooking Reduces Pesticide Residues — Boiling, Blanching, Stir-Frying (2022)

*"Boiling, Blanching, and Stir-Frying Markedly Reduce Pesticide Residues..." — PMC9141337. Tested 88 pesticides on Chinese kale and yard-long beans by GC-MS/MS.*

## Reduction by method
| Vegetable | Boiling | Blanching | Stir-frying |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chinese kale | 18–71% | 36–100% | 25–60% |
| Yard-long beans | 38–100% | 27–28% | 35–63% |

## Key points
- **Boiling/water-based methods** are most effective on water-soluble (low-lipophilicity) pesticides; **lipid-soluble** compounds (chlorpyrifos, flumethrin) resist removal (confidence: medium).
- **Blanching removed 100%** of some compounds (alachlor, captan) — and blanching is exactly what commercial frozen-veg processors do before freezing.
- Reduction is large but **incomplete and pesticide-dependent** — cooking is not a substitute for choosing low-residue produce.

## Why it matters for the stir-fry question
Frozen stir-fry blends are **commercially washed and blanched before freezing**, then the consumer **stir-fries or steams** them — a second residue-reducing step. The pesticide load you actually eat is therefore **lower than the raw-produce residue figures** that drive the EWG list. This is a real argument for *not* paying the organic premium on a blend you're going to cook.

> [!gap] Study used Asian leafy greens/beans, not the exact Costco blend; the *direction* (cooking reduces residue) generalizes, but exact percentages do not.
