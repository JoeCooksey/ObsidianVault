# Use of Non-Sugar Sweeteners: WHO Guideline (2023)

The single most consequential official document on this topic. Released **15 May 2023**, built on a WHO-commissioned systematic review (Rios-Leyvraz & Montez 2022) of 283 studies.

## The Recommendation
> WHO suggests that **non-sugar sweeteners (NSS) not be used as a means of achieving weight control or reducing the risk of noncommunicable diseases** (conditional recommendation).

Covers all synthetic and natural NSS (aspartame, acesulfame-K, sucralose, saccharin, stevia, etc.) in manufactured foods/beverages — i.e. the sweeteners in nearly every diet soda.

## The Reasoning (this is the crux of the whole topic)
- **The evidence splits by study design.** Randomized controlled trials (short-term) show a modest reduction in body weight/BMI when NSS replace sugar. Prospective cohorts (long-term) show the *opposite* — higher NSS intake is associated with increased **type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and all-cause mortality**. (confidence: high — this is WHO's own summary)
- WHO concluded NSS **"do not confer any long-term benefit in reducing body fat"** in adults or children.
- The recommendation is **conditional** because the long-term harm signal comes from observational data where **reverse causation and confounding cannot be ruled out** (people already gaining weight or at metabolic risk switch to diet drinks). See [[Reverse Causation in Sweetener Cohort Studies]].

## Scope / Caveats
- **Excludes people with pre-existing diabetes** — the guideline does not tell diabetics to abandon NSS.
- It is a recommendation about NSS as a *health strategy*, **not a safety warning** and **not a claim that NSS are toxic**. WHO's framing: swapping sugar for sweetener is not a free win; the better long-term move is to cut sweetness overall.
- Contested: some nutrition scientists (e.g. *Eur J Clin Nutr* 2023 rebuttal) argue the guideline over-weights confounded cohort data over cleaner RCTs.
