# Daniel & Woody 2010 — "They Hear, but Do Not Listen"

*Teaching of Psychology*, 37(3), 199–203. The classic negative result for podcast learning — and the boundary condition for it.

## Method

College students received the same primary-source material either as a podcast or as printed text, then took a comprehension quiz. Preferences and study habits were also assessed.

## Findings

- **The podcast group performed worse than the text group** on the quiz (secondary summaries report roughly a 28% lower average, medium confidence — figure not verified against the paywalled original)
- Students **preferred** the podcast format going in; the preference reversed immediately after the quiz — a clean demonstration of the fluency illusion (listening *feels* easier, which is exactly why it teaches less here)
- Authors' conclusion: podcasts can supplement or enrich, but are "not as effective as text for delivering primary content"

## Why it conflicts with later studies — and why it doesn't

[[Jabbari 2025 — Podcast Learning While Driving RCT]] and Willingham's synthesis find audio ≈ text in many conditions. The reconciliation (see [[Learning from Podcasts (Evidence Review)]]): this study used **dense primary-source text studied for a graded quiz** — exactly the condition where reading's affordances (self-pacing, re-reading, seeing structure) matter most. The aging of the study (2010, pre-modern podcast production) is a second caveat. Mark as potentially stale per program rules, but the complexity boundary it demonstrates has held up.
