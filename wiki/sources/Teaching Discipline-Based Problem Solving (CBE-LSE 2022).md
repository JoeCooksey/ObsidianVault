# Teaching Discipline-Based Problem Solving (CBE-LSE 2022)

An evidence review essay synthesizing decades of discipline-based education research on how to teach problem solving in STEM.

## What it contributes — the two instructional sequences

**I→PS (Instruction then Problem Solving):**
- *Peer-Led Team Learning* — lecture then small-group problem sets; improves exams, lowers fail/withdraw rates, especially for underserved students (Frey 2018).
- *[[Worked Examples and Productive Failure|Worked examples + practice]]* — demonstrate full solutions, then practice; cuts extraneous load for novices; good for **near transfer**, weaker for far transfer (Kalyuga 2001; Atkinson 2000).

**PS→I (Problem Solving then Instruction):**
- *POGIL* (guided inquiry) — beats lecture for reasoning (Vincent-Ruz 2020).
- *Contrasting cases* — comparing problems that differ in key features helps students spot deep structure; best **before** instruction (Alfieri 2013) → see [[Analogical Encoding (Comparing Cases)]].
- *[[Worked Examples and Productive Failure|Productive failure]]* — attempt hard problems first, then instruction; worse short-term, better long-term (Kapur 2008).

## Anchor findings

- Expert/novice gap traces to **schemas in long-term memory** (Chase & Simon 1973; Chi 1981; Larkin 1980).
- **Desirable Difficulties** (Schmidt & Bjork 1992): calibrated struggle improves retention and transfer. See [[Desirable Difficulties]].
- **Near vs far transfer** (Barnett & Ceci 2002): far transfer needs conceptual abstraction; **abstraction learners outperform memorizers on far-transfer** (McDaniel 2018). See [[Near vs Far Transfer]].
