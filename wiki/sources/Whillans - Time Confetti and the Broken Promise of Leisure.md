# Whillans — Time Confetti and the Broken Promise of Leisure

Essay by Harvard Business School professor [[Ashley Whillans]] (*Behavioral Scientist*, 2020), adapted from her book *Time Smart*. It explains why modern people feel chronically short on time despite having more leisure hours than prior generations.

## What it contributes

The central diagnosis for the topic "what to do instead of killing time": the problem is rarely a *shortage* of free minutes — it is the **fragmentation and contamination** of the minutes you have. The fix is structural (defend continuous, uninterrupted blocks), not just motivational.

## Key claims

- **"Time confetti"** (term coined by [[Brigid Schulte]], popularized by Whillans) = the little bits of seconds and minutes lost to unproductive multitasking and interruption. (confidence: high)
- One hour of leisure interrupted by **14 notifications loses ~10% of the time** to the interruptions alone — and, worse, chops the rest into unusable 5–6 minute fragments. (confidence: high)
- The **"autonomy paradox"**: technology meant to grant flexibility instead lets work intrude on leisure continuously, so you are never fully off. (confidence: high)
- Fragmentation taxes you cognitively because each interruption reminds you of everything else you could or should be doing — so even uninterrupted scraps feel anxious rather than restful. (confidence: high)
- People **estimate they had *less* free time than they actually did** — time confetti makes us feel more time-impoverished than we are. (confidence: medium — self-report)
- **Time affluence is trainable like fitness**: small deliberate daily steps (15 min of guitar, 10 min meditating) beat waiting for a rare big block. (confidence: medium)

## Limits

Popular essay, not a single study — synthesizes Whillans's own research program plus others'. The notification arithmetic is illustrative. Treat the mechanism as high confidence and the exact percentages as indicative.
