# Mann & Cadman 2014 — Does Being Bored Make Us More Creative?

*Creativity Research Journal*, by [[Sandi Mann]] and Rebekah Cadman (University of Central Lancashire). The most-cited empirical support for the "boredom breeds creativity" claim.

## Key findings

- Participants who first did a boring task (copying numbers from a phone book) generated **more creative uses for plastic cups** than controls. A second experiment found *reading* the phone book (more passive, more daydream-permitting) beat *writing* from it. (confidence: medium — single lab, modest samples)
- Proposed mechanism: boredom invites **daydreaming / mind-wandering**, which feeds divergent thinking. Related lab work: a 2013 study found mild boredom primes convergent problem-solving; bean-sorting-by-color before an ideation task improved idea generation (covered by [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/creativity-mental-benefits-boredom)).

## The contradiction — read before citing

A **2016 study found boredom probably does not generate creativity** — practice does — and in some cases boredom *impaired* creative performance (per National Geographic's review). Philosopher Andreas Elpidorou's framing reconciles it: any benefit depends on your *response* to boredom, not the boredom itself — "I wouldn't praise the boredom for that; I would praise you."

> [!gap] Treat "boredom causes creativity" as **medium-confidence and contested**. The safer claim: boredom creates an opening that *can* be used for constructive mind-wandering — if you don't immediately kill it with stimulation.

## Mann's practical advice (from *The Upside of Downtime*)

To harness the daydreaming state: pick an activity needing little or no concentration — walk a familiar route, swim laps, sit with eyes closed — **without phone or music**, and let the mind wander. "If you want your mind to wander, switching off your phone is a must."
