# Di Chiara and Imperato 1988 — Drugs of Abuse and Dopamine

*"Drugs abused by humans preferentially increase synaptic dopamine concentrations in the mesolimbic system of freely moving rats."* PNAS 85(14):5274–5278. University of Cagliari. The landmark microdialysis study that established the dopamine theory of addiction.

## What It Contributes

Using in-vivo brain microdialysis, the authors showed that structurally unrelated **drugs abused by humans** — opiates, ethanol, nicotine, amphetamine, cocaine — all raise extracellular dopamine, **especially in the nucleus accumbens** (a limbic/reward area) more than in the dorsal caudate (motor area). This common action on the **mesolimbic pathway** is the foundation of the idea that addictive drugs "hijack" the reward system.

This is the **primary-literature root** of the popular activity/drug multipliers in [[Dopamine Increases by Activity (Tier List)]] — the rat-microdialysis fold-increases that were later simplified for public communication (e.g., amphetamine producing the largest spike, far above food rewards).

## Confidence Note

Confidence **high**: foundational, heavily replicated, peer-reviewed (PNAS). Caveat: it is a **rat** study — the magnitudes do not transfer one-to-one to human dopamine increases, which is why downstream "multipliers" are directional rather than exact.
