# How to Take Smart Notes — Sönke Ahrens (2017)

The best-known guide to the **Zettelkasten** ("slip-box") method developed by sociologist [[Niklas Luhmann]]. Ahrens's contribution was to systematize and proceduralize Luhmann's workflow into something actionable for any reader or writer.

## The Core Argument

Most reading produces nothing because notes are made *about* a book in isolation and then filed away to die. The slip-box flips this: every reading session feeds a single growing network of ideas in your own words. Writing is not the last step after thinking — **writing notes IS the thinking**, and a good note system means you never face a blank page.

## The Note Pipeline

| Stage | What it is | Lifespan |
|-------|-----------|----------|
| **Fleeting note** | A quick capture while reading/living — a scribble, a margin mark | Discarded within 1–2 days after processing |
| **Literature note** | Short summary in *your own words* of what a source said, with the citation | Kept in a reference manager (Zotero/Mendeley) |
| **Permanent note (Zettel)** | One atomic idea, self-contained, written "as if for print," linked to other notes | Never discarded |
| **Project note** | Notes assembled outside the slip-box for a specific piece of writing | Project-bound |

## Rules That Make It Work

- **Always read with a pen.** Reading without capturing is entertainment.
- **Literature notes must be selective and in your own words.** Transcribing is not understanding; the test is rephrasing. (Source: zettelkasten.de explainer)
- **Permanent notes are atomic** — one idea per note, written so it stands alone with no need to revisit the source.
- **Elaboration is the point.** The act of asking "How does this connect to what I already have?" is what drives learning and recall.
- **Link aggressively.** A note that isn't linked into the web is effectively lost — the network, not the individual note, is the asset.
