# Cognitive Training — A Field in Search of a Phenomenon

**Published:** *Perspectives on Psychological Science* (2023), summarizing the skeptical consensus. Anchors the broader debate alongside Simons et al. (2016) and Au et al. (2020).

The single most important counterweight to "play this game and your IQ goes up."

## Key Findings
- **Meta-analyses do not show reliable FAR transfer** from cognitive/brain training to untrained abilities like fluid intelligence. (confidence: high)
- Gains are mostly **near transfer** — you get better at the trained task and very similar tasks, not at general reasoning.
- **When ACTIVE control groups are used** (controls do an engaging alternative, not nothing), far-transfer effects shrink toward null. Apparent gains in poorly-controlled studies are largely placebo / expectancy / test-retest practice.
- Working-memory-training → fluid-intelligence literature is **underpowered**, with small-study effects inflating estimates (Au et al.; Melby-Lervåg).
- Commercial brain-training claims (e.g., Lumosity's FTC settlement) outran the evidence.

## Contribution to Topic
Frames the honest answer to "which game increases IQ": **purpose-built brain-training apps mostly DON'T transfer to general intelligence.** This is why entertainment games that demand deep, varied engagement (Portal 2 beating Lumosity; MOBAs tracking fluid intelligence) tend to look better than dedicated trainers — and why the safest claim is "trains specific cognitive skills," not "raises IQ."

> [!gap] This is the mainstream skeptical position; some 2025 gamified-training studies report far transfer in narrow domains (e.g., simulated flight). General-IQ far transfer remains unproven.
