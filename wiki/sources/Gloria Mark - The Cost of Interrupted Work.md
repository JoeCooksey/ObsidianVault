# Gloria Mark - The Cost of Interrupted Work

CHI 2008 paper (plus Mark's broader shadowing studies) establishing the empirical cost of being interrupted at work. The foundational source for the **[[Interruption Resumption Cost]]** concept.

## Key claims

- After an interruption it takes an average of **23 minutes 15 seconds** to fully return to the original task. (confidence: high — most-cited figure in interruption research; note it is an average across resumed work, not a fixed per-interruption penalty)
- People compensate for interruption by working **faster** — but pay in **higher stress, frustration, time pressure, and effort**. (confidence: high)
- ~**82%** of interrupted work is resumed the same day, but typically only after **2+ intervening activities**. (confidence: high)
- **Resumption lag** — the reorientation needed to rebuild the mental context of the suspended task — is the mechanism; each interruption taxes working memory. (confidence: high)

## What it contributes to the topic

It supplies the **selection rule** for a low-demand job: if every hour brings a customer interruption, the cost falls hardest on tasks with high resumption lag (deep, cognitively demanding, single-thread work) and barely touches tasks with low resumption lag (passive input, atomic chunks, routine admin). So the question "what should I do between calls?" reduces to "what is **[[Fragmentation-Tolerant Activities|fragmentation-tolerant]]**?"

## Caveats

> [!gap] The "23 minutes" figure is an aggregate from office-task observation; it is routinely over-quoted as if every micro-interruption costs 23 minutes. Treat it as evidence that resumption is *costly and non-trivial*, not as a precise per-call tax.
