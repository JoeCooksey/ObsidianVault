# Meta-Analyses of Nonsugar Sweeteners (Azad 2017 + Toews 2019)

Two pooled analyses that establish the "the evidence is genuinely weak and split" baseline underneath the WHO guideline.

## Azad 2017 (CMAJ) — According to PubMed
- 7 RCTs (1,003 participants, median 6 mo) + 30 cohorts (405,907 participants, median 10 yr).
- **RCTs: no significant effect on BMI** (mean difference −0.37 kg/m², CI −1.10 to 0.36).
- **Cohorts: NNS associated with higher BMI, weight, waist circumference, and higher incidence of obesity, hypertension, metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular events.**
- **Publication bias detected** for diabetes outcomes. Conclusion: RCTs "do not clearly support the intended benefits" and observational data "may be associated with increased" risk.
- [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1503/cmaj.161390)

## Toews 2019 (BMJ) — the WHO-commissioned review — According to PubMed
- 56 studies (35 observational). In adults, **very-low/low-certainty** small benefits on BMI (−0.6) and fasting glucose (−0.16 mmol/L); lower NSS doses linked to less weight gain.
- **For most outcomes, no difference** between NSS use and non-use, and **no benefit for people actively trying to lose weight**.
- Overriding message: studies are **small, short, low-quality** — "confidence in the reported results is limited."
- [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.k4718)

## What They Mean
- The honest state of the field: **no strong evidence of benefit, no strong evidence of harm.** The signal on both sides is small and confounded. This is exactly why official recommendations conflict.
