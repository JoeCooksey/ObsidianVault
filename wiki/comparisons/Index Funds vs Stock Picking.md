# Index Funds vs Stock Picking

> The evidence stack runs three layers deep: professionals fail (SPIVA), the math is rigged against picking (Bessembinder), and retail investors do worse than the professionals (Barber & Odean).

## Head-to-Head

| Dimension | Index Funds | Individual Stocks |
|-----------|------------|-------------------|
| Expected outcome | Market return minus ~0.03% | Market return minus costs minus behavior gap; small chance of far better |
| Diversification | Instant (3,600–9,000 companies) | DIY; most retail portfolios are badly undiversified |
| Time required | ~0 hr/yr | Research + earnings + monitoring, ongoing |
| Cost | 0.00–0.10% ER | $0 commissions, but spreads, taxes on turnover, and mistakes |
| Volatility | Market-level | Far higher per holding |
| Tax | Low turnover = efficient; ETFs best in taxable | Turnover triggers gains; per-lot tax-loss harvesting possible |
| Skill ceiling | None — you get the market | Real but vanishingly rare; must beat people doing this full-time |
| Emotional load | Low (nothing to decide) | High (disposition effect, FOMO, panic) |

## The Evidence Stack

**1. Professionals lose to the index.** 79% of active large-cap US funds underperformed the S&P 500 in 2025 alone — the fourth-worst year in SPIVA's 25-year history; ~92% underperform over 20 years; after 15 years there is *no category* where a majority of active managers win (Source: [[SPIVA Scorecard — Active vs Index]]). (confidence: high)

**2. The math is structurally against picking.** Net of T-bills, *all* US stock market wealth creation 1926–2016 came from the top ~4% of stocks; the other ~96% collectively matched T-bills, and 4 of 7 stocks ever listed returned *less* than T-bills over their lifetime (Source: [[Bessembinder - Do Stocks Outperform Treasury Bills]]). A concentrated portfolio that misses the few extreme winners doesn't slightly lag — it misses the entire equity premium. See [[Skewness of Stock Returns]]. (confidence: high)

**3. Retail investors underperform even further.** Individual investors lag benchmarks by ~3.6%/yr after costs; the most active 20% of traders earned 11.4%/yr vs 18.5% for buy-and-hold; the stocks individuals buy subsequently underperform the ones they sell (Source: [[Barber and Odean - Individual Investor Performance]]). (confidence: high)

## Pros of Picking Stocks (the honest list)

- **Possibility of outperformance** — an index fund can never beat its index; a picker can. The base rate is just poor.
- **Genuine edge from specific knowledge** — domain expertise (e.g., Joe's EE/semiconductor depth) can occasionally produce real insight before the market prices it. Edge must be *information or analysis the market lacks*, not enthusiasm.
- **Tax-loss harvesting per position** — individual lots give more tax flexibility than a single fund.
- **Engagement and learning** — following companies teaches accounting, valuation, and industry dynamics; for some this is the point.
- **Control and conviction** — no forced ownership of companies you dislike.

## Cons of Picking Stocks

- Must overcome all three evidence layers above simultaneously.
- Time cost is real and recurring (earnings seasons, filings, theses).
- Concentration cuts both ways — single-company risk (fraud, disruption, key-person) is uncompensated risk you could diversify away for free.
- Behavioral taxes compound: disposition effect, overtrading, attention-driven buying.

## The Resolution

This is not actually a binary. The evidence-consistent positions are:

1. **100% index** — mathematically dominant default for most people.
2. **[[Core-Satellite Portfolio]]** — index core (80–90%+) with a capped stock-picking satellite. Keeps the compounding engine safe while preserving learning, engagement, and the lottery ticket on real edge.

What the evidence does *not* support: a majority-individual-stock portfolio as the wealth-building plan.
