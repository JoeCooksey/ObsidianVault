# Fyodor Dostoevsky

**Born**: November 11, 1821, Moscow, Russian Empire
**Died**: February 9, 1881, St. Petersburg
**Known for**: *[[Crime and Punishment - Fyodor Dostoevsky|Crime and Punishment]]* (1866), *The Idiot* (1869), *Demons* (1872), and *The Brothers Karamazov* (1880)

---

## Background

Dostoevsky is one of the towering figures of Russian and world literature, famous for probing the darkest corners of human psychology, faith, and moral freedom. His fiction stages ideas as lived crises inside real, contradictory people rather than arguing them abstractly.

A defining event shaped his worldview: in 1849 he was arrested for belonging to a liberal intellectual circle, sentenced to death, and led before a firing squad — only to be reprieved at the last moment and sent to a Siberian prison camp for four years. The mock execution, the years of hard labor, his lifelong epilepsy, a ruinous gambling addiction, and deep Orthodox Christian faith all feed directly into his novels' obsession with [[Redemption Through Suffering|suffering, guilt, and redemption]].

He wrote in fierce opposition to the [[Russian Nihilism|nihilist]] and [[Rational Egoism|rational-egoist]] ideologies sweeping 1860s Russia, arguing that reason detached from faith and love leads to spiritual catastrophe — the central drama of [[Rodion Raskolnikov|Raskolnikov's]] story.

---

## Core Ideas

- **[[Redemption Through Suffering]]** — moral regeneration comes through accepting suffering, confession, and love, not through logic
- **[[The Extraordinary Man Theory]]** — dramatized (and demolished) in *Crime and Punishment* as a spiritual poison
- **Critique of [[Rational Egoism]] and [[Russian Nihilism]]** — reason without conscience destroys the self and society
- **[[Psychology of Guilt]]** — conscience as an inescapable inner tribunal, more punishing than any court
