# Aspartame

The most-studied and most-litigated artificial sweetener; the classic sweetener in Diet Coke and Diet Pepsi. A dipeptide (aspartic acid + phenylalanine), ~**200× sweeter than sugar**, first marketed in US carbonated beverages in **1983**.

## Key Facts
- Metabolized to aspartate, phenylalanine, and methanol — **not "inert,"** but at doses far below toxic thresholds. (Warning label matters only for **phenylketonuria (PKU)** sufferers, who can't metabolize phenylalanine.)
- **Acceptable Daily Intake**: 40 mg/kg/day (JECFA/EFSA), 50 mg/kg/day (US FDA) = **~9–14 cans of diet soda/day** for a 70 kg adult.
- **IARC July 2023**: classified **Group 2B, "possibly carcinogenic to humans"** — based on *limited* evidence (hepatocellular carcinoma). Same tier as aloe vera and pickled vegetables. See [[IARC-JECFA 2023 Aspartame Assessment]].
- **JECFA July 2023**: reaffirmed the ADI; **no safety concern** at real-world intake. FDA disputed IARC's classification.

## Study Signal
- **Weakest** of the common sweeteners on the gut microbiome / glycemic front — did **not** impair glucose tolerance in [[Suez 2022 - Sweeteners Alter Human Glucose Tolerance (Cell)|Suez 2022]].
- Cohort ([[Debras 2022 - Artificial Sweeteners and Cardiovascular Disease (NutriNet-Sante)|Debras 2022]]) linked aspartame to a small increase in **cerebrovascular (stroke)** risk — observational, subject to [[Reverse Causation in Sweetener Cohort Studies|reverse causation]].

## Bottom Line
The cancer headline is about **hazard, not risk**: at any realistic diet-soda intake, aspartame is regarded as safe by every major food-safety regulator. The metabolic case against it is weaker than for sucralose or saccharin.
