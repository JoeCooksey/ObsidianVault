# Higher Order Thinking

**Higher order thinking (HOT)** is reasoning about how pieces of information *relate to and influence each other* — contextual, multifactorial, high-complexity problem solving where there is no single right answer — as opposed to **lower order thinking**, which handles facts in isolation (memorizing, understanding, recalling, regurgitating). [[Justin Sung]] frames it as the single skill that keeps a knowledge worker competitive as AI improves, because it is precisely the kind of work today's [[Large Language Models|LLMs]] (transformer architecture) are worst at. (Source: Justin Sung, *How To Learn So Fast That AI Can Never Replace You*, YouTube, 2026 — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-Xc_ExgwLs8)

## The core argument

- Businesses hire and retain people on the **belief that they can solve future, partially-undefined problems** — not on the static skills they hold today. Reports (Deloitte/LinkedIn, Gallup) estimate **30–50% of in-demand workforce skills will change in ~5 years**, so the durable asset is the *ability to learn and integrate*, not the current skill set. This is the same thesis as [[Learning Agility (Meta-Skill)]].
- **Humans and AI are not in the same race.** AI getting better requires the *underlying technology* to advance; a human getting better just requires applying the brain they already have in the right direction. So a human can out-improve AI at HOT without waiting on any breakthrough.
- Using AI only to **speed up your own workflow is the same reasoning a business uses to replace you** — if your 40-hour job becomes a 10-hour job, the question becomes why pay you 40, then why pay you at all. The defensible move is to occupy the work AI can't do: deeply contextual, high-stakes, see-how-it-all-fits-together problem solving.
- The value of lower order thinking is **capped and falling** (AI already exceeds most humans at it); the value of higher order thinking **rises with skill** because you can take on bigger, messier responsibilities others can't.

## The non-obvious insight: there is no ladder from lower to higher order

The familiar pyramid (à la Bloom's taxonomy) implies you climb from memorization up to synthesis. Sung argues from coaching tens of thousands of people that **this is wrong and counterproductive**: lower and higher order thinking are *different habits*, and getting better at memorizing never converts into the ability to integrate. You only build HOT by **deliberately doing HOT even while bad at it** — it will not arrive on its own. The prerequisite mindset: **a fact has value only through its relationships to other facts**, never in isolation, so attention should go to unraveling connections.

This reframes much of the vault's learning cluster: [[Active Recall (Retrieval Practice)]], [[Spaced Repetition]], and the [[Generation Effect (Handwriting)]] are *memory* engines (necessary, lower-order-adjacent); HOT is the *integration* engine layered on top. See the two-engine model in [[Research: How to Learn Anything (The Science of Learning)]].

## How to build it — three practices

1. **Apply from day zero.** Don't try to understand or memorize new material first — immediately ask *"how does this apply to a problem I'm actually solving?"* Understanding then happens as a byproduct. Rule of thumb: if more than **5–10 minutes** pass between taking in information and asking how to use/connect it, the brain prunes it as valueless. Always learn *against a real problem or goal*. (This is the practical core of [[Reading Application Framework|ARIA]] and the build-to-learn pattern.)
2. **Build mental organization, not physical organization.** Mental organization = grouping and relating ideas so you see the whole picture and could explain it simply. Tool: **mind mapping** — and when a connection turns out wrong or a new factor appears, *revise the map*; discovering the map is wrong is the goal, not a failure. If mapping feels "too complex to even start," that is the signal you don't yet see the structure and need to do it more.
3. **Forget perfection — think on paper.** ~70% of people freeze when mapping because there are too many candidate connections. Fix: stop deciding in your head and **draw candidate links on paper** (A→B? A→C?), look at them, regroup, redraw. The paper is a thinking *assistant*, not a source of truth. Sung calls this the single biggest unlock for the largest number of people.

## The trap directly relevant to a second brain

Sung explicitly names **color-coded notes, perfect tags, flashcards, citation software, the immaculate Notion/Obsidian template** as *physical organization* — and labels it **"productive procrastination"**: it produces the *feeling* of organization without the integrative thinking that creates value. The warning lands on any elaborate note system (this vault included). The HOT-aligned use of a wiki is not to perfect its structure but to **force connections** — make explicit claims about how concepts relate (the wikilinks themselves), then revise them as understanding matures. This is the same failure mode as [[Cognitive Offloading (Learning Risk)]]: the system can do the *filing* as long as the human keeps doing the *thinking*.

## Why Joe cares

This is the "what to aim learning *at*" companion to the vault's "how to learn" cluster. It argues the AI-proof target is integration and judgment — the same conclusion as [[Learning Agility (Meta-Skill)]] and [[Programming Skills AI Cannot Replace]] (architecture/debugging/taste outlast syntax). Concrete drill: pick one real problem, open a blank canvas, dump the relevant points, and force the connections — revising as you go. That exercise *is* the skill.
