# Company Investment Due Diligence Master Guide

The complete framework for evaluating any publicly traded company before investing. Five stages, in order — skip none.

## The 5-Stage Framework

| Stage | Question | Key Tools |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Business Understanding | What does this company actually do? | 10-K Business section, investor presentations |
| 2. Financial Health | Are the numbers solid and improving? | 3 financial statements, key ratios |
| 3. Competitive Position | Can this company defend its profits? | Moat analysis, Porter's Five Forces |
| 4. Management Quality | Do I trust the people running this? | Proxy statement, insider ownership, capital allocation |
| 5. Valuation | Am I paying a fair price? | DCF, EV/EBITDA, P/E vs. peers |

**Rule**: Form your own view of the business from the 10-K and earnings transcripts — read the investor presentation LAST. Management is selling you a story; the raw filings are the facts.

---

## Stage 1: Business Understanding

Before any numbers, understand the business model in plain English.

**Questions to answer:**
- What product or service does the company sell?
- Who are the customers? (consumer, enterprise, government)
- How does it make money? (subscription, transaction, licensing, physical product)
- What is the unit economics? (revenue per customer, gross margin per unit)
- What are the growth drivers?
- Who are the main competitors?

**Where to find it:** 10-K Part I, Item 1 (Business). The first 10–20 pages describe the business in full. Also: the company's investor relations page for their latest presentation.

**Rule:** If you cannot explain the business model in two sentences, do not invest. "I don't understand it but smart people like it" is not an investment thesis.

## Stage 2: Financial Health

Three statements, read together. See [[Financial Statement Analysis for Investors]] for the full deep dive.

### The 3 Financial Statements

**Income Statement** — Is the company profitable and growing?
- Revenue growth rate (YoY %)
- Gross margin (Revenue − COGS) / Revenue
- Operating margin (EBIT / Revenue)
- Net income + EPS trend

**Balance Sheet** — Is the company financially stable?
- Cash and equivalents vs. total debt
- Debt-to-Equity ratio
- Current ratio (current assets / current liabilities) ≥ 1.5 is healthy
- Goodwill / intangibles relative to total assets (danger if very high)

**Cash Flow Statement** — Is the company generating real cash?
- Operating cash flow (OCF) — must be positive and growing
- Free Cash Flow = OCF − Capital Expenditures
- FCF yield = FCF / Market Cap (>5% is attractive)
- FCF vs. Net Income — they should roughly track; big divergence = red flag

### Key Ratios to Check First
See [[Key Financial Ratios for Investors]] for all 15 ratios.

| Ratio | What It Tells You | Good Benchmark |
|---|---|---|
| ROIC | Value creation per dollar invested | >10% (>15% = excellent) |
| ROE | Profit per dollar of shareholder equity | >15% sustained |
| Gross Margin | Pricing power + product strength | Depends on industry |
| D/E Ratio | How leveraged is the company | <1.0 for most industries |
| Free Cash Flow Yield | Cash generation relative to price | >5% = attractive |

## Stage 3: Competitive Position (The Moat)

A great business is one that earns high returns on capital for a long time. That requires a moat — a durable structural advantage. See [[Economic Moat Framework]] for full analysis.

### The 5 Sources of Moat (Morningstar)
1. **Network Effects** — product gets more valuable as more people use it (Visa, Meta, Airbnb)
2. **Switching Costs** — customers are trapped by cost/pain of leaving (Salesforce, Adobe, Oracle)
3. **Cost Advantages** — structurally cheaper to produce than competitors (Amazon warehouses, Costco buying power)
4. **Intangible Assets** — brands, patents, regulatory licenses (Coca-Cola brand, pharma patents, FAANG data moats)
5. **Efficient Scale** — market is only big enough for one or two players (pipelines, utilities, niche industrial)

**Moat test (financial):** ROIC > 15% sustained for 5+ years = moat is real. A company that earns high returns will attract competition; only a moat explains why those returns persist.

**Moat width:**
- **Wide moat** — advantage lasts 20+ years (Berkshire's bar)
- **Narrow moat** — advantage lasts 10+ years
- **No moat** — profits will be competed away; avoid unless extremely cheap

### Porter's Five Forces
Assess the industry structure, not just the company:
1. **Rivalry intensity** — how hard does the industry compete on price?
2. **Threat of new entrants** — how hard is it for a new company to enter?
3. **Threat of substitutes** — can customers switch to a different product entirely?
4. **Supplier power** — do suppliers extract most of the value?
5. **Buyer power** — do customers have leverage to push prices down?

Best industries: high barriers to entry, no substitutes, fragmented suppliers, loyal customers. Worst: airlines, retail, commodity producers.

## Stage 4: Management Quality

Great businesses can be destroyed by bad management. Bad businesses can be turned around by great management.

**What to evaluate:**

**Capital Allocation:** What does management do with the cash the business generates?
- Best: reinvest at high ROIC + return surplus via buybacks/dividends
- Worst: dilutive acquisitions, empire-building, excessive executive compensation
- Red flag: frequent large acquisitions that destroy shareholder value

**Insider Ownership:**
- Founders/management owning meaningful stake (5%+) = skin in the game
- Insider buying > insider selling is a positive signal
- Heavy insider selling before bad news = major red flag
- Check: SEC Form 4 filings at EDGAR

**Track Record:**
- Has management hit past guidance?
- What do employees say? (Glassdoor CEO approval ratings)
- Have they been honest about mistakes in earnings calls?
- Do they explain failures clearly, or deflect?

**Compensation:**
- Is pay tied to long-term value creation (ROIC, FCF) or just EPS and stock price?
- Excessive stock option grants dilute shareholders
- Base salary as % of operating income — if very high, they're taking shareholder money

**Where to find it:** DEF 14A (Proxy Statement) — filed annually, discloses full executive compensation and insider ownership. 10-K "Management" section. Earnings call transcripts (SeekingAlpha free, Motley Fool, or company IR page).

## Stage 5: Valuation

Even the best company can be a bad investment if you overpay. See [[Stock Valuation Methods]] for full methods.

### The Two Approaches

**Intrinsic Valuation (DCF):**
Forecast future free cash flows and discount them back to today at the company's cost of capital (WACC).
- Theoretically correct — what the company is actually worth
- Very sensitive to assumptions (growth rate, discount rate)
- Use as a sanity check, not gospel

**Relative Valuation (Multiples):**
Compare the company's valuation to peers using ratios.

| Multiple | Formula | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| P/E | Price / Earnings | Most common; use for profitable companies |
| EV/EBITDA | Enterprise Value / EBITDA | Capital structure-neutral; prefer over P/E for debt-heavy companies |
| P/S | Price / Revenue | Early-stage or unprofitable growth companies |
| P/B | Price / Book Value | Banks, financials, asset-heavy companies |
| FCF Yield | FCF / Market Cap | Mature cash-generating businesses |

**Simple valuation checklist:**
1. What are peers trading at? Is this company a discount or premium — and why?
2. What growth rate does the current price imply? Does that seem realistic?
3. What's the downside scenario? If growth slows, how far does the stock fall?
4. What is the margin of safety? (Buffett: buy at a discount to intrinsic value)

**The Buffett Rule:** Better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price.

## One-Page Summary

Before buying any stock, answer these 10 questions:

1. Can I explain what this company does in 2 sentences?
2. Is revenue growing consistently (>5%/yr)?
3. Are margins expanding or contracting?
4. Is FCF positive and growing? Does it match net income?
5. What is the moat source and how durable is it?
6. Is ROIC > cost of capital (>10%)?
7. Is the balance sheet manageable (D/E < 1.5)?
8. Does management have skin in the game?
9. Is the stock trading at a reasonable multiple vs. peers?
10. What is my thesis and what would cause me to sell?
