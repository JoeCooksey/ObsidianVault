# Stock Investing Red Flags

Warning signs in financial statements, management behavior, and corporate governance that signal a stock may be dangerous to hold. These are patterns that precede fraud, poor performance, or outright collapse.

**Rule:** One red flag is a question. Two or more red flags in the same company = exit or avoid.

---

## Category 1: Financial Statement Red Flags

### The #1 Red Flag: Earnings ≠ Cash Flow
**Signal:** Net income is positive and growing, but Free Cash Flow (OCF − Capex) is flat, declining, or negative.
**Why it matters:** Earnings can be manipulated through accounting; cash cannot. A persistent gap between reported profits and cash generation is the most reliable warning sign of accounting issues.
**What to check:** On the cash flow statement, compare OCF to Net Income over 3–5 years. They should roughly track. Divergence → investigate what's absorbing cash (receivables? inventory? deferred revenue reversals?).

### Revenue Growing Faster Than Accounts Receivable
Wait — the red flag is actually the REVERSE: **receivables growing faster than revenue**.
**Why:** If the company is recognizing revenue before collecting cash, they may be booking fake or premature revenue. Compare A/R growth rate to revenue growth rate on the balance sheet.

### Gross Margin Compression
**Signal:** Revenue grows but gross margins are falling.
**Why it matters:** Means the company is losing pricing power or input costs are rising uncontrollably. The business is becoming commoditized. Over time, this leads to margin squeeze all the way to the bottom.

### Non-GAAP Adjustments That Never End
**Signal:** Company reports "adjusted EPS" or "adjusted EBITDA" that perpetually excludes "non-recurring" charges that actually recur every year.
**Examples:** Restructuring charges every year, stock-based compensation (real economic cost to shareholders, not "non-cash" from the shareholder's perspective), "one-time" integration costs from acquisitions
**Rule:** If it happens every year, it's not non-recurring. Look at GAAP numbers; non-GAAP is management's preferred reality.

### Inventory Building Without Revenue Growth
**Signal:** Inventory on the balance sheet grows significantly while revenue is flat or declining.
**Why:** Products not selling. Could signal demand problems, obsolescence, or channel stuffing (pushing inventory to distributors to book as "sales").

### Goodwill Impairment Charges
**Signal:** Company takes a large write-down on goodwill.
**Why:** Goodwill = premium paid on acquisitions. Impairment means management is admitting they overpaid and the business they acquired isn't worth what they said. Serial acquirers often accumulate massive goodwill then take impairment charges during downturns.

### Auditor Issues
**Signal:** Qualified opinion, adverse opinion, or going-concern warning from the auditor.
**A going-concern warning** = auditors are not confident the company will survive 12 months. This is rare and serious — the auditors are saying the lights might go out.
**Frequent auditor changes** = companies sometimes fire auditors who disagree with their accounting. Investigate why.

### Rising Debt + Rising Reported Earnings
**Signal:** Company borrows more while reporting rising earnings.
**Why:** Debt can temporarily inflate earnings (buy back shares, boosting EPS mechanically; make acquisitions that appear accretive). Real earnings growth comes from the business, not from financial engineering.

## Category 2: Management Red Flags

### Heavy Insider Selling
**Signal:** Multiple executives or directors selling large blocks of stock simultaneously.
**Context matters:** A single executive selling to diversify is normal. Multiple insiders selling at the same time before a negative announcement = major red flag.
**Where to check:** SEC Form 4 filings (OpenInsider.com aggregates these clearly).

### Low or Declining Insider Ownership
**Signal:** CEO/founders hold <1% of the company or have sold most of their stake.
**Why:** Founders/management with significant personal wealth tied to the stock price are aligned with shareholders. When they've sold most of their stake, their incentives may diverge.
**Exception:** Very large companies where even 0.1% = massive wealth.

### Management Guidance Misses Repeatedly
**Signal:** Company consistently misses its own guidance.
**Why:** Either management doesn't understand their own business or they're setting high guidance to inflate the stock price. Check: has guidance been raised or lowered quarter-over-quarter over the past 2 years?

### Defensive or Evasive Earnings Call Behavior
**Signal:** Management dismisses or talks around specific analyst questions. Overly defensive responses. "We don't guide on that metric."
**Why:** Honest management teams answer tough questions directly or say "I don't know." Defensiveness on straightforward questions suggests they don't want you to know the answer.
**Compare:** The best management teams discuss failures openly and explain what went wrong. The worst never acknowledge mistakes.

### Excessive and Opaque Executive Compensation
**Signal:** Large executive compensation disconnected from company performance; high base salaries as % of operating income; compensation structure tied to short-term EPS rather than long-term ROIC/FCF.
**Where to check:** DEF 14A (Proxy Statement) — full compensation tables disclosed annually.
**Red flag combo:** Layoffs announced while executive bonuses increase.

### Serial Acquirer With Poor Track Record
**Signal:** Company makes frequent acquisitions, often at high premiums; goodwill grows as a large % of total assets; post-acquisition performance disappoints.
**Why:** Many CEOs build empires through acquisitions (fees, status, power) that destroy shareholder value. Check: for each major acquisition, did the combined entity outperform expectations 3 years later?

### Changing Accounting Methods or Reporting Segments Frequently
**Signal:** Company shifts how it defines revenue, changes reporting segments, or changes key metrics it reports to investors.
**Why:** Consistent reporting lets investors track performance over time. Changes often obscure a deteriorating segment or metric.

## Category 3: Corporate Governance Red Flags

### Dual-Class Share Structures Without Founder Performance
**Signal:** Founders hold super-voting stock that gives them control even as their economic ownership shrinks.
**Why it matters:** This is not inherently bad (Google, Meta early stage), but if the company is underperforming and founders cannot be voted out, minority shareholders have no recourse.

### Board Lacks Independence
**Signal:** Board is packed with personal friends of the CEO, former executives, or people with financial relationships to the company.
**Why:** An independent board is supposed to represent shareholders. A captured board rubber-stamps management decisions even when harmful to shareholders.
**Where to check:** DEF 14A — board member biographies and any disclosed relationships.

### Related-Party Transactions
**Signal:** Company does significant business with entities controlled by executives or their families.
**Examples:** CEO's private company gets contracts from the public company; executives' family members on payroll at inflated compensation.
**Where to find:** Notes to financial statements in the 10-K; "Related Party Transactions" section in the proxy statement.

### Frequent Restatements of Prior Earnings
**Signal:** Company revises its previously reported financial results.
**Why:** Restatements indicate that prior numbers were wrong — either due to mistakes or intentional misrepresentation. Frequent restatements = do not trust the numbers.

### High Short Interest
**Signal:** > 15–20% of shares outstanding sold short by institutional investors.
**Why:** Sophisticated short sellers have done deep research and are betting the company is overvalued or fraudulent. High short interest is not a buy signal (short sellers can be wrong) but warrants investigation.
**Where to check:** Finviz.com → stock page → "Short Float" percentage.

## Final Rule: The Charlie Munger Inversion Test

"Tell me where I'm going to die and I won't go there."

Before investing in any company, list the ways this investment could fail:
1. What would cause this business to be disrupted in 5 years?
2. What happens if management is dishonest?
3. What happens if the economy turns down significantly?
4. What is the worst-case scenario for the balance sheet?

If the answers are catastrophic and the company shows any of the above red flags — the margin of safety is not worth it.
