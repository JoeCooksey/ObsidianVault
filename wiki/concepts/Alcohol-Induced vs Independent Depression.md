# Alcohol-Induced vs Independent Depression

When someone quits drinking and looks depressed, there are **two distinct possibilities** — and the difference matters because they have different outcomes and treatments.

## 1. Alcohol-induced (alcohol was *causing* the low mood)
Alcohol is a central-nervous-system depressant. Heavy use disrupts mood chemistry, so the drinking itself drives the depression. **Tell-tale sign: mood lifts substantially within ~3–4 weeks of abstinence.** If he feels meaningfully better in the first few weeks, the alcohol was likely the cause, and continued sobriety is the cure. (Source: [[Alcohol and Depression — Healthline]].)

## 2. Independent / self-medicated (depression came *first*)
Often the depression was there first and the drinking arose to numb it — **self-medication.** Alcohol is a "Band-Aid"; once sober, the Band-Aid is ripped off and the underlying depression is **unmasked**, raw and visible. Tell-tale sign: low mood **persists or deepens** well past the first month and doesn't follow the PAWS-recovery curve. This kind does **not** resolve on its own — it needs treatment (therapy and/or medication). (Source: [[Alcohol and Depression — Healthline]].)

> [!key] You often can't tell which it is up front — **time is the diagnostic.** Weeks 1–4 of sobriety reveal a lot: clear improvement points to alcohol-induced; persistent or worsening depression points to an independent disorder that needs professional care.

## Dual diagnosis and the safety flag
When depression and alcohol problems co-occur ("dual diagnosis"), **both must be treated** — sobriety alone won't fix an independent depression. Critically, co-occurring depression and alcohol use carries an **elevated suicide risk**, so depressive symptoms in early sobriety must be taken seriously, not waited out indefinitely. See [[Crisis and Help Resources (Mental Health and Substance Use)]].
