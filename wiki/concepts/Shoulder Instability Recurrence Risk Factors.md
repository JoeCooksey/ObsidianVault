# Shoulder Instability Recurrence Risk Factors

The single most useful question for "will this keep happening / do I need surgery" is **how high is the recurrence risk** for this specific shoulder. These factors drive both the natural risk of re-dislocation and the failure risk of a repair.

## Factors that raise recurrence
- **Young age** — under ~20–25 is the strongest predictor; risk climbs steeply the younger you are.
- **Male sex.**
- **Contact / collision / overhead sports** (rugby, football, wrestling, throwing).
- **Glenoid bone loss (bony Bankart)** and a **large/engaging Hill-Sachs** lesion.
- **Generalized ligamentous laxity / hyperlaxity** (e.g. external rotation >90°).
- **Number of prior dislocations** — each event causes more damage. Operating after **2+ dislocations carries ~3.8× the failure odds** vs fixing it after the first. (Source: [[Gasparini et al 2015 — Predictors of Recurrence After Arthroscopic Stabilization]])

## The "more dislocations = worse" loop
Every dislocation can enlarge the labral tear and erode glenoid bone, which both **raises the chance of the next one** and **lowers the success rate of an eventual repair** (and pushes the choice toward a bone procedure, [[Latarjet Procedure]]). This is the core argument against indefinitely "living with it" once recurrence is established. (Source: [[Gasparini et al 2015 — Predictors of Recurrence After Arthroscopic Stabilization]])

## Formal scoring
- **Instability Severity Index Score (ISIS)** — a pre-op score (age, sport type/level, hyperlaxity, Hill-Sachs and glenoid contour on X-ray) that estimates recurrence risk after arthroscopic Bankart repair and flags when a Latarjet may be better. Calculated by a surgeon from history + imaging.

> [!gap] No score replaces imaging-measured bone loss and an in-person exam; ISIS and "glenoid track" assessment guide, not dictate, the operation choice.
