# Seed Oils Health Debate

"Seed oils" (canola, soybean, sunflower, corn, grapeseed, etc.) are the subject of a loud online claim that their **omega-6 linoleic acid causes inflammation and chronic disease**. The weight of evidence from major health bodies does **not** support that claim.

## What the evidence actually shows
- **Cardiovascular / mortality:** A Harvard analysis of 38 studies and >810,000 people found the **highest** linoleic-acid intake was associated with **13% lower** all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. (Source: [[AHA 2024 — No Reason to Avoid Seed Oils]])
- **Diabetes:** Higher linoleic-acid levels were tied to ~35% lower risk of developing type-2 diabetes, plus improved glucose metabolism. (Source: [[AHA 2024 — No Reason to Avoid Seed Oils]])
- **Inflammation claim debunked:** A 2012 meta-analysis of 15 RCTs found **no** increase in inflammatory or oxidative-stress markers with higher linoleic-acid intake. (Source: [[AHA 2024 — No Reason to Avoid Seed Oils]])

## Where the organizations stand
- **American Heart Association** (2024): no reason to avoid seed oils; omega-6 fats are beneficial to the heart.
- **Harvard Health:** no need to avoid healthy omega-6 fats.
- **Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health** (2025): evidence does not support the harm narrative; the real concern is that seed oils are concentrated in **ultra-processed foods**.

## The honest nuance
> [!gap] The science is "no evidence of harm," not "proven perfectly safe at any dose." Some Mendelian-randomization and RCT data are mixed on whether high omega-6 levels actively *benefit* the heart, and the debate is not formally closed. (Source: STAT News, 2025)

- **The confounder that matters most:** seed oils are ubiquitous in fried fast food, chips, and packaged snacks. People eating lots of seed oil are often eating lots of [[Food Health Tier List — Macronutrients|ultra-processed food]] — the likely culprit, not the oil itself.
- **Oxidation is the real cooking risk**, and it applies to *all* high-PUFA oils: reused, overheated, or rancid oil produces harmful aldehydes regardless of the seed-oil debate. (See [[Oxidative Stability of Cooking Oils]].)

## Practical takeaway
You don't need to fear seed oils used sensibly. But **extra-virgin olive oil remains the better default** for everyday cooking — more stable, more antioxidants, and the strongest long-term health track record. Whole foods cooked at home beat the seed-oil-laden processed versions either way.
