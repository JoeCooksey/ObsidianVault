# Domain-Specific Knowledge vs General Problem-Solving Skill

The central debate in how problem-solving ability actually works — and the answer reshapes how you should train.

## The strong domain-specific position

There is **no content-free "problem-solving skill"** you can train abstractly and deploy anywhere. Performance depends overwhelmingly on **domain-specific knowledge in long-term memory**. Tricot & Sweller call it "arguably the most important factor, and possibly the only factor, determining acquired cognitive performance." (Source: [[Neelen & Kirschner 2018 — Domain-Specific Knowledge 1, Generic Skills 0]])

Evidence: chess masters win by recognizing positions, not by general reasoning; experts use schemas while novices fall back on weak general methods. General critical-thinking/problem-solving courses reliably fail to transfer.

## The reconciliation (what's actually true)

The strong position overcorrects. The accurate synthesis:

1. **Domain knowledge is the dominant factor.** Without schemas there is nothing for a strategy to operate on. ~80% of the work is just *knowing the domain deeply*.
2. **Process knowledge helps — but only on top of domain knowledge.** [[Pólya's Problem-Solving Method|Pólya's heuristics]], [[Metacognition in Problem Solving|metacognition]], and [[Analogical Encoding (Comparing Cases)|comparing cases]] genuinely improve problem solving *within a domain you already know*. They are force multipliers, not substitutes.
3. **General strategies transfer only when taught with self-monitoring and applied across varied contexts** — and even then transfer is hard and limited. (Source: [[Teaching Discipline-Based Problem Solving (CBE-LSE 2022)]])

## The practical consequence

> [!tip] One-line rule
> "Becoming a better problem solver" = getting deep in specific domains **plus** internalizing a transferable process. You can't skip the domains.

This is why brain-training games don't work ([[Near vs Far Transfer]]) and why the fastest route to "better at problem solving" is to pick real domains (math, programming, your field) and solve real problems in them. It also reframes [[Higher Order Thinking]]: higher-order skill is built *on* a body of knowledge, not instead of it.
