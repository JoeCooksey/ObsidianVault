# Good Explanations (Hard-to-Vary)

## Definition
An **explanation** is the account you give of how and why something is the case — including the stories you tell about who you are. A **good explanation is "hard to vary"**: its parts are tightly constrained by what it's trying to explain, so it survives criticism and pressure. A **bad explanation is easy to vary** — you can swap its details freely without losing anything — so it collapses the moment the right pressure is applied in the right spot. The "hard to vary" criterion comes from physicist **[[David Deutsch]]** (*The Beginning of Infinity*); the framing here is from **[[Craig Perry]]**'s video.

## Why It Matters
Perry's central claim is that **the quality of your explanations determines almost all outcomes in your life**, because every perception, decision, and self-judgment is filtered through one. The danger is that **bad explanations are invisible** — they don't announce themselves, they just "feel like the way things are," so their only symptom is the suffering they quietly cause. And they're **free**: rebuilding an explanation costs nothing, needs no tools, credentials, or permission, and can be done anywhere, anytime — the highest-leverage, lowest-cost intervention available. Example: arriving at a party where you know no one can be explained as "a bad situation, hide until friends arrive" *or* "a chance to meet new people" — same experience, two explanations, opposite lives.

## How It Works
- **Test by varying.** A good explanation resists arbitrary change; if you can rewrite its details and it still "works" equally well, it explains nothing.
- **Apply pressure deliberately.** The [[Explanation-Building Writing Practice]] operationalizes this: write the explanation down to make it visible, then **find one piece of evidence that challenges it** — to stress-test, not to destroy.
- **Completeness via quadrants.** An explanation is only as good as the number of [[Four Quadrants (AQAL)|quadrants]] you've actually checked it against; most people stop at one.
- **Awareness sets the ceiling.** The [[Nine Levels of Thinking|level of thinking]] you operate from determines which explanations you can even perceive — lower levels can't see the better explanations available above them.

## Cross-Domain Connections
- Sits in the same family as the scientific method: conjecture + criticism (**[[David Deutsch]]**, building on Popper's falsifiability).
- Complements cognitive reframing in psychology — but the "hard-to-vary" test is an epistemic standard, not just positive thinking.
- The "stories you tell yourself" angle echoes **[[Carl Jung]]**'s warning that if you don't define yourself, the world will.
