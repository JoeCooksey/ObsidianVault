# Oxidative Stability of Cooking Oils

**Oxidative stability** is an oil's resistance to chemical breakdown when exposed to heat, oxygen, and moisture. It — not [[Smoke Point (Cooking Oils)|smoke point]] — is the property that determines how much harmful material an oil produces during cooking.

## What makes an oil stable
1. **Low polyunsaturated fat (PUFA).** PUFAs have multiple double bonds that oxidize easily when heated; they are the fragile part of any oil. EVOO is ~8–14% PUFA, vs canola ~28% and grapeseed ~70% — which is why grapeseed degrades fast despite a decent smoke point. (Source: aboutoliveoil.org)
2. **High monounsaturated + saturated fat.** These resist oxidation; olive (mono-rich) and tropical/animal fats (saturated) are stable.
3. **Natural antioxidants.** Polyphenols and secoiridoids in EVOO actively delay oxidation; refining strips them, so refined oils lose this protection. (Source: [[Foods 2025 — Smoke Point Predictive Models for Virgin Olive Oils]])
4. **Minimal refining + freshness.** Old, light-exposed, or already-oxidized oil starts degraded.

## What degradation produces
When oil is heated repeatedly with food moisture and air, it undergoes hydrolysis, oxidation, and polymerization, generating **total polar compounds (TPC)**, trans fats, and harmful aldehydes. TPC is the standard industry marker of oil degradation — frying oil is rejected once it exceeds ~24–27% TPC. (Source: ScienceDirect frying-oil studies)

> [!tip] In controlled heating tests, EVOO produced **lower** polar compounds and trans fats than canola, grapeseed, and rice bran oil — i.e., it degrades *less*, not more. (Source: [[De Alzaa 2018 — Chemical and Physical Changes in Oils During Heating]])

## Frying-life ranking (rough)
- **Most stable:** palm, high-oleic sunflower, EVOO, refined avocado, peanut (high mono/sat, lower PUFA).
- **Least stable:** grapeseed, regular sunflower, soybean, rice bran, flax/walnut (high PUFA — never for high heat).
- High-oleic sunflower is specifically bred for frying stability; regular (high-linoleic) sunflower is much less stable. (Source: ScienceDirect, high-oleic sunflower frying study)

## Practical implications
- For **heat**, choose low-PUFA oils; save high-PUFA oils (flax, walnut, grapeseed, regular sunflower) for [[Finishing Oils (Cold-Use Oils)|cold use]].
- **Never reuse frying oil** — each cycle drops stability and raises polar compounds.
- Store oils cool, dark, and sealed; light and air oxidize them before they ever hit the pan.
