# Staying On Top of News — Complete Guide

Master hub for building a sustainable, impartial news diet. See also [[Impartial News Sources Tier List]], [[Media Literacy and Bias Detection]], [[News Tools and Apps Stack]].

---

## The Core Problem

Most people consume news passively and reactively. Research shows:
- **70% of news consumption** happens through passive social media scrolling
- Passive consumption produces **lower retention and higher anxiety** vs. intentional reading
- Algorithms optimize for **engagement, not comprehension** — outrage and fear drive clicks
- Average person: **2.5 hours/day** consuming negative news content
- **>30 min/day** of news consumption is linked to measurable anxiety increases

The goal is not to consume *more* news — it is to consume *better* news, intentionally, from a curated set of sources, in controlled windows.

## Step-by-Step Daily News Routine

### Step 1 — Curate Your Source Stack (One-Time Setup)

Build a focused list, not a massive one:

| Category | Target Count | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Tier-1 wire/public (factual baseline) | 1–2 | Reuters, AP, PBS NewsHour |
| National outlet (depth) | 1–2 | BBC, NPR, The Economist |
| Domain-specific (your field) | 1–3 | IEEE Spectrum, Ars Technica, Nature News |
| Local news | 1 | Your city's main paper or public station |

**Total: 5–8 sources maximum.** Reading 10–15 individual newsletters = 60–90 min/day — unsustainable and counterproductive.

Use [[News Tools and Apps Stack]] to subscribe via RSS (Feedly or Inoreader) so all sources flow into one feed.

### Step 2 — Set a News Window (Daily)

Pick **one or two fixed windows** per day. Do not check outside them:
- **Morning window (15–20 min)**: headlines + one deep read
- **Evening window (optional, 10–15 min)**: scan anything you missed

Total: **30 min/day maximum.** This is consistent with the research threshold for anxiety. More time does not equal better-informed.

### Step 3 — Choose Your Consumption Format

| Format | Best Use | Time Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Morning briefing email (Axios AM, NYT Morning) | Fast daily summary | 5 min |
| RSS feed (Feedly + curated sources) | Controlled, algorithm-free reading | 15–20 min |
| AI digest (Particle, Readless) | Fastest multi-source catch-up | 5–10 min |
| Long-form podcast (BBC Global News, NPR Up First) | Commute/exercise | 15–30 min |
| Social media | **Avoid as primary source** | Variable → infinite |

**Rule**: Social media is a pointer to stories, not a story source. When you see something on social, go verify it at the primary outlet directly.

### Step 4 — Apply the Cross-Check Rule

For any story that matters or surprises you:
1. Find it on **at least 3 sources** from different points on the political spectrum (use [[Ground News]] or [[AllSides]])
2. Check the outlet's bias rating at **mediabiasfactcheck.com**
3. Verify any specific factual claims at Snopes, PolitiFact, or FactCheck.org
4. Ask: *Is this a news story (what happened) or an opinion/analysis piece?* Opinion framed as news is one of the most common bias vectors

### Step 5 — Protect Against Filter Bubbles

Algorithms learn what you engage with and feed you more of it — creating an echo chamber. Counter-strategies:

- **Use Ground News**: shows you who is and isn't covering a given story (blind-spot detector)
- **Rotate perspectives weekly**: deliberately read one outlet that challenges your assumptions
- **Don't "like" or engage** with news on social media — this feeds the algorithm
- Check [[AllSides]] monthly to verify your source stack isn't drifting one direction

### Step 6 — Limit Doomscrolling

- Remove news apps from your phone's home screen; access via browser only (friction = lower passive use)
- Turn off all breaking news push notifications
- Set a phone screen timer for 30 min/day on news apps
- If a story is triggering anxiety: **close it, don't scroll further** — you will not solve it by reading more

## Key Mental Models

**Consume news like a scientist, not a fan.** A fan wants confirmation. A scientist wants the most accurate model of reality. Hold your sources lightly and update your views.

**The overnight test**: If a story will not matter to you in 24 hours, it does not need 30 minutes of your attention today.

**Breaking news is the least accurate news**. Initial reports on fast-moving events are frequently wrong. Wait 24–48 hours before accepting a narrative on a major breaking story.

**Headlines ≠ articles**. Studies show 59% of shared news articles are shared without being read. Always read the full piece before forming an opinion or sharing it.
