# MS EE Return on Investment

The financial case for a master's in electrical engineering. **Verdict: high ROI** for most career goals.

## The Numbers

- **Salary premium:** 15–25% over a bachelor's; ~18% on average. Worth $300,000+ over a career. (Source: [[Source - edX Is a Master's in EE Worth It]]) `confidence: high`
- **Concrete gap:** master's median weekly earnings $1,840 vs. bachelor's $1,543 in 2024 (~$15,500/yr).
- **Entry-level MS EE:** ~$85K; **median MS EE:** ~$100K.
- **Cost:** ~$20K–$70K total. On-campus ~$21,072/yr; online ~$13,688/yr.
- **Payback period:** 3–7 years, depending on job market, location, and whether the program is subsidized.

## What Raises the ROI

1. **Employer tuition sponsorship** — collapses the cost side to near zero.
2. **Funded TA/RA or fellowship** — ~40% of MS students get tuition waivers/stipends via teaching or research roles.
3. **Prestigious / research-focused program** — better employer networks and access to specialized, higher-paying roles.
4. **High-return specialization** — power systems, semiconductor design, communications, control engineering beat generalist tracks.
5. **Integrated 4+1 (BS/MS)** — adds ~1 year instead of 2, improving the math.

## When It's Worth It

- Aiming for **lead engineer, engineering manager, or R&D lead** roles.
- **Specializing** into a niche (signal processing, power systems, AI hardware).
- **Pivoting** into a fast-growing area (renewables, biomedical, telecom).

## When to Skip / Defer It

- You only want to practice general engineering — a bachelor's enters most EE fields (see [[EE Career Fields by Degree Level]]).
- You'd pay full freight with no employer help and no funded spot — the payback still works but is slower.
