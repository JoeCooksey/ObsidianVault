# The Extraordinary Man Theory

The central idea [[Rodion Raskolnikov|Raskolnikov]] lays out in his article "On Crime" in *[[Crime and Punishment - Fyodor Dostoevsky|Crime and Punishment]]*: humanity divides into **"ordinary"** people, who exist to obey and reproduce, and **"extraordinary"** people — Napoleons, lawgivers, prophets — who have the right to **transgress moral law** and even shed blood if their idea demands it. For the extraordinary man, murder in service of a great enough purpose is not a crime.

Raskolnikov murders the pawnbroker partly to test which category he belongs to — is he a Napoleon, or merely a "louse"? The novel is Dostoevsky's demolition of the theory: the idea is airtight on paper and lethal in a living conscience. Raskolnikov cannot bear what he has done, which proves he was never "extraordinary." [[Arkady Svidrigailov|Svidrigailov]] is the theory taken to its logical end — a man genuinely beyond good and evil — and he is a suicidal void, not a hero. The theory is a distant cousin of Nietzsche's later *Übermensch* and a direct product of the [[Rational Egoism|rational-egoist]] and [[Russian Nihilism|nihilist]] currents Dostoevsky was writing against.

*Source: [[Crime and Punishment - Fyodor Dostoevsky]].*
