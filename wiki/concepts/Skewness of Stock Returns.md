# Skewness of Stock Returns

> Individual stock returns are extremely right-skewed: the *average* market return is created by a tiny number of huge winners, while the *typical* stock is a loser. This single statistical fact is the deepest reason stock picking is hard and indexing works.

## The Core Fact

Long-horizon individual stock returns do not cluster around the market average — they pile up near zero/negative with a long thin tail of enormous winners:

- **4 out of 7** US stocks ever listed (1926–2016) had lifetime buy-and-hold returns *below one-month T-bills* (Source: [[Bessembinder - Do Stocks Outperform Treasury Bills]]) (confidence: high)
- The top **~4% of stocks** account for *all* net wealth creation above T-bills; the other 96% collectively just matched T-bills (confidence: high)
- Just **86 stocks** produced half of all US market wealth creation over 90 years; globally, 2.4% of firms produced the entire $75.7T of net wealth 1991–2020 (confidence: high)

## Why This Breaks Stock Picking

The market's ~10%/yr average is a **mean dragged up by outliers**, not a typical outcome. Consequences:

1. **A random concentrated portfolio most likely lags the index** — the median stock loses to T-bills, so a handful of picks probably contains zero of the rare mega-winners.
2. **Missing winners is catastrophic, not marginal** — skip the next Apple/NVIDIA and there is no "average" stock that makes it back; the equity premium *lives in the tail*.
3. **The index holds every future winner by construction** — indexing is not settling for average; it is a guaranteed claim on the outliers nobody can reliably identify in advance.

This reframes diversification: it's not just risk reduction, it's **tail capture**.

## Connection to Picking Skill

Bessembinder: poorly diversified active strategies "most often underperform" — picking winners "resembles a lottery more than a systematic advantage." Even genuinely skilled pickers face a base-rate problem: the prior that any given stock is a long-run winner is well under 50%.

> [!gap] The skewness argument says picking is *hard*, not impossible — Bessembinder himself notes the same math implies enormous payoffs for anyone who *can* identify tail firms early. No reliable public method for doing so survives scrutiny ([[Efficient Market Hypothesis]]).
