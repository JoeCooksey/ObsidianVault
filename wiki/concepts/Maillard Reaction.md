# Maillard Reaction

The **Maillard reaction** is the chemistry of browning: amino acids (from protein) react with reducing sugars under heat to create hundreds of new aroma and flavor compounds — the "savory, nutty, roasted" taste of seared meat, toast, coffee, and crusty bread. It is the single biggest lever for making cooked food *taste* better.

## The conditions it needs

- **Heat above ~300°F (150°C).** Below this, you get poaching/steaming, not browning. (Source: [[Ground Beef Browning Science]])
- **A dry surface.** Water boils off at 212°F and holds the surface there; browning can't start until the surface is dry. This is why crowding the pan (trapped steam) and wet meat both kill browning.
- **Time at temperature.** A few minutes of undisturbed contact builds the crust.
- **Alkalinity accelerates it.** A higher-pH surface browns faster and deeper — the basis of the [[Ground Beef Browning Science|baking-soda trick]] and of pretzels (lye dip). (Source: ATK; [[ATK — Baking Soda for Ground Beef]])

## Maillard vs caramelization

Different reactions, often confused:
- **Maillard** = amino acids + sugars (needs protein). Meat, bread crust, roasted coffee.
- **Caramelization** = sugars alone breaking down (~340°F+). Onions, caramel, roasted vegetables.

Both produce brown color and rich flavor; most browned foods involve both.

## The trade-off: flavor vs. heat-formed toxins

Aggressive high-heat browning of meat also produces [[Heat-Formed Food Toxins (HCAs, PAHs, AGEs, Acrylamide)|HCAs, PAHs, and AGEs]]. The practical balance: brown for flavor, but avoid charring to carbon. (Source: [[Heat-Formed Food Toxins (HCAs, PAHs, AGEs, Acrylamide)]])

> [!note] Eggs are the exception. Great scrambled eggs are cooked *gently and below* browning temperature on purpose — browning eggs makes them tough, gray, and sulfurous. The Maillard reaction is the goal for [[Ground Beef Browning — Masterclass|ground beef]] and the enemy for [[Scrambled Eggs — Masterclass|scrambled eggs]].
