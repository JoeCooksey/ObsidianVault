# Sell Discipline (When to Sell a Stock)

> The hardest and most neglected half of investing. Most people obsess over what to buy and sell by emotion. A written sell rule, followed unemotionally, is what separates results from luck.

## The Principle

Define your sell process *before* you buy, make it match your buy thesis, and execute it without emotion — especially under market stress. "Every great investor sells differently because every great investor buys differently." (Source: Cabot, Motley Fool, Kiplinger, RuleOneInvesting) (confidence: high)

One study found funds that **sell at a predetermined target price** earned ~1.23% average monthly returns (≈14.8% annualized). (confidence: medium)

## Valid Reasons to Sell

1. **The thesis broke** — the specific reasons you bought no longer hold (lost moat, management change, deteriorating fundamentals). The single most legitimate sell.
2. **Target price reached** — you calculated a fair/"sticker" price before buying; the market hit it. Take the gain.
3. **Rebalancing** — a winner has grown into an oversized share of the portfolio, concentrating risk; trim back to target weight.
4. **Defensive sell** — protect capital and gains when a holding starts weakening on the technicals. Used regularly by disciplined traders.
5. **Changed personal circumstances** — cash need, or risk tolerance dropped (e.g., nearing retirement).

## The Worst Reason to Sell

**"The price dropped."** It is the #1 reason most people actually sell — fear kicks in, they hit sell to stop the feeling — and it is usually wrong. Selling a sound business into a panic locks in a loss and forfeits the recovery. (confidence: high)

> Missing just the 10 best S&P 500 days from 2000–2020 cut returns from ~6% to ~2.4% — and those best days clustered right after the worst days. Panic sellers miss the rebound. (cross-ref [[Index Fund Investing]], [[Research - Financial Literacy Roadmap]])

## The Behavioral Core

This is where the **disposition effect** bites: investors sell winners too early (to "lock in" a gain) and hold losers too long (to "avoid" realizing a loss) — exactly backwards. A written rule overrides the instinct.
