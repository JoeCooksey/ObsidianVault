# Book List — Career and Leadership

Books for building a career, not just getting a job. Covers: how to become excellent, how to communicate that excellence, how to navigate organizations, and how to develop the leadership capabilities that separate top engineers from average ones.

## Why This Category

Technical skill is necessary but not sufficient. The engineers who build careers vs. just jobs are those who communicate well, understand organizational dynamics, and develop leadership capability. The books here bridge the gap between technical mastery and professional impact.

## S-Tier

### So Good They Can't Ignore You — Cal Newport (2012)
Newport's career philosophy book, deliberately written as an argument against "follow your passion." Instead: build rare and valuable skills (career capital), then use them to get compelling work.

- Core thesis: passion comes **after** mastery, not before; "follow your passion" is bad career advice for most people because it requires passion to precede skill
- Career capital theory: the best careers require rare, valuable skills; becoming excellent gives you leverage to negotiate for autonomy, mission, and impact
- The craftsman mindset: focus on becoming so good at what you do that they can't ignore you
- **Joe relevance**: directly validates the WBG power electronics specialization approach; build the rare skill first, the interesting career follows
- **Read this**: before deciding on any major career pivot or questioning your path

### The Pragmatic Programmer — Andrew Hunt & David Thomas (1999, updated 2019)
The software craftsman's Bible. Despite being a "software book," almost every principle applies to any technical engineering work.

- Core principles:
  - **DRY** (Don't Repeat Yourself) — in code, documentation, and processes
  - **Orthogonality** — reduce dependencies between components
  - **Tracer bullets** — build working end-to-end slices before optimizing any one part
  - **Your knowledge portfolio** — treat skills like financial investments; diversify, invest regularly, rebalance
  - **Care about your craft** — the relentless pursuit of doing it right
- **Joe relevance**: every EE who writes code (which is now every EE) benefits from this framework; it's also the best guide to developing a professional engineering mindset

### On Writing Well — William Zinsser (1976, 30th anniversary edition 2006)
The definitive guide to clear non-fiction writing.

- Core thesis: the secret of good writing is **rewriting** — first drafts are for discovering what you think; revisions are for communication
- Principles: strip unnecessary words ruthlessly; be specific not general; use active voice; write to be understood, not to impress
- **Joe relevance**: engineers who write clearly advance 2–3 career levels faster than peers of equal technical ability; emails, documentation, proposals, and reports are all writing; this skill compounds over a career

## A-Tier

### The Manager's Path — Camille Fournier (2017)
The technical career ladder from individual contributor (IC) to engineering director, with a chapter on each level.

- Covers: being a junior engineer → senior engineer → tech lead → manager → manager of managers → VP
- Core lesson: each level requires fundamentally different skills, not just more of the same skills; the jump from IC to manager is the hardest and most commonly failed transition
- **Joe relevance**: read before first internship; understand the career ladder you're entering from the beginning; the tech lead chapter alone is worth the book

### Radical Candor: Be a Kick-Ass Boss Without Losing Your Humanity — Kim Scott (2017)
Care personally + challenge directly = Radical Candor. The 2×2 matrix: ruinous empathy (care without challenge), obnoxious aggression (challenge without care), manipulative insincerity (neither), radical candor (both).

- Practical communication framework applicable immediately as a student and intern
- The feedback formula: situation + behavior + impact; be specific, not general
- **Joe relevance**: giving and receiving feedback is a daily skill in technical teams; knowing the framework from year one means arriving at internships already thinking in professional terms

### A Guide to the Good Life: The Ancient Art of Stoic Joy — William Irvine (2008)
Modern Stoicism guide — more practical and accessible than Meditations, with specific exercises and modern applications.

- Covers: negative visualization, internalized goals vs. externalized outcomes, voluntary discomfort, the trichotomy of control
- Core exercise: **negative visualization** — periodically contemplate loss (of a project, a relationship, a skill) to build resilience and appreciation
- The dichotomy of control (Epictetus): focus exclusively on what is within your control; release what isn't; this eliminates anxiety from external outcomes
- **Joe relevance**: stress management for high-stakes situations (internship interviews, exams, ASU career fairs); the Stoic frame reduces anxiety to manageable signal

### The First 90 Days — Michael Watkins (2003)
Acceleration guide for new roles — how to ramp up faster than average in a new job, internship, or project.

- The 5 types of transitions (startup, turnaround, realignment, sustaining success, new company)
- Securing early wins: identify a 90-day milestone that demonstrates competence; deliver it
- Learning plan: what do I need to learn, by when, from whom?
- **Joe relevance**: apply this to every internship; arriving with a learning plan in day 1 is visibly unusual and memorable

## B-Tier

### How to Solve It — George Polya (1945)
The mathematician's guide to problem-solving. Covers heuristics for attacking unfamiliar problems.

- The 4 steps: understand the problem → devise a plan → carry out the plan → look back
- Heuristics: draw a diagram, solve a simpler version, work backwards, find analogous problems
- Despite being written about math, this is a general-purpose problem-solving framework
- **Joe relevance**: every unfamiliar EE problem can be attacked with Polya's framework; having this explicit vocabulary makes you more systematic

### The Effective Engineer — Edmond Lau (2015)
Leverage-focused productivity for engineers: focus on high-leverage activities, invest in iteration speed, validate early, improve estimation.

- Core concept: leverage = impact / time; obsess over high-leverage tasks
- **Joe relevance**: directly applicable to portfolio building; same time investment, different project choices = dramatically different career outcomes

## Joe's Career-Specific Reading Priority

Given Joe's profile (EE freshman, power electronics track, target LLNL/Sandia, 19 years old):

1. **NOW**: *So Good They Can't Ignore You* — validates the specialization strategy
2. **Before first internship**: *The First 90 Days* — arrive with a plan
3. **During first internship**: *The Pragmatic Programmer* — professional engineering habits
4. **Before senior year**: *The Manager's Path* — understand the career ladder
5. **Any time**: *On Writing Well* — the compounding communication investment
