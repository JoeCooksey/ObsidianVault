# Near vs Far Transfer

**Transfer** = applying what you learned on one problem to a different one. The single most important — and most disappointing — fact about skill building.

## The two kinds

- **Near transfer** — to similar problems in the same domain (a slightly different physics problem). Happens readily.
- **Far transfer** — to dissimilar problems or other domains (chess skill → business strategy). **Rare and hard; usually doesn't happen.** (Source: [[Teaching Discipline-Based Problem Solving (CBE-LSE 2022)]])

## The brain-training verdict

Working-memory and "brain training" programs produce near transfer (you get better at the game) but **no reliable far transfer** to fluid intelligence or general reasoning, per an 87-study meta-analysis. There is no general mental muscle to train. (Source: [[Melby-Lervag Redick Hulme 2016 — WM Training No Far Transfer]])

## What actually produces (some) far transfer

Transfer is the exception, but it can be coaxed:

1. **Learn principles, not facts.** Rote learning blocks transfer; understanding the underlying structure enables it.
2. **Compare multiple examples** to extract the shared schema → [[Analogical Encoding (Comparing Cases)]]. Two source analogs beat one.
3. **Vary the contexts** you practice in (don't always practice the same way) → [[Interleaving (Learning)]].
4. **Abstraction over memorization** — learners who abstract the principle beat those who memorize exemplars on far-transfer tests (McDaniel 2018).
5. **Teach the strategy with explicit self-monitoring** and cues for *when* to apply it.

> [!tip] One-line rule
> Don't chase a magic general skill. Build deep domain skill (near transfer is real), and force abstraction to buy what little far transfer is available.
