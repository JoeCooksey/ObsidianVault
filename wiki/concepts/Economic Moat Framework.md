# Economic Moat Framework

An economic moat is a durable competitive advantage that protects a company's profits and market share from competitors over time. The term was coined by Warren Buffett — a great business without a moat will have its profits competed away within a decade.

**The central question:** Why can't competitors just copy this and take the business?

**Financial test:** A company with a real moat will sustain ROIC > 15% for 5+ consecutive years. High returns attract competition; only a moat explains why those returns persist.

---

## The 5 Sources of Moat (Morningstar Framework)

### 1. Network Effects
The product becomes more valuable as more people use it. Each additional user adds value for all existing users.

- **Examples:** Visa/Mastercard (more merchants → more cardholders → more merchants), Meta (more friends on platform = harder to leave), Airbnb (more hosts → more travelers → more hosts), marketplaces
- **How to identify:** Does adding one more user make the product meaningfully better for everyone else?
- **Strength:** Can be extremely durable — network effects create winner-take-all dynamics
- **Weakness:** Can collapse rapidly if a better network forms (MySpace → Facebook)

### 2. Switching Costs
Customers are trapped — the cost, risk, or inconvenience of switching to a competitor is high enough that they stay even if the product isn't the best.

- **Examples:** Salesforce/Oracle/SAP (enterprise software deeply embedded in workflows), Adobe Creative Suite (years of training investment), banks (pain of changing accounts), cloud providers (data migration)
- **How to identify:** How much would it cost a customer (in money, time, re-training) to switch? If the answer is "a lot," switching costs are real
- **Strength:** Creates reliable recurring revenue and pricing power
- **Weakness:** Can be overcome by extremely superior products (see: Microsoft Office vs. cloud alternatives eroding slowly)

### 3. Cost Advantages
The company can structurally produce its product or service cheaper than any competitor — not because of temporary luck, but because of scale, process, location, or unique resources.

- **Examples:** Costco (buying power + bare-bones operations), Amazon warehouses (logistical scale), Southwest Airlines (single aircraft type, low overhead), commodity miners with uniquely low-cost deposits
- **How to identify:** Does this company have a structural reason to be cheaper — not just "we're working hard"?
- **Types:** Scale advantages (biggest wins on unit economics), proprietary resources (unique mine, water rights), process innovation (Toyota Production System)
- **Weakness:** Technology shifts can destroy cost advantages (e.g., legacy manufacturing disrupted by automation)

### 4. Intangible Assets
Brand recognition, patents, regulatory licenses, or proprietary data that competitors cannot easily replicate.

- **Brand:** Coca-Cola (emotional loyalty + global distribution), LVMH (luxury identity), Apple (ecosystem + status signal)
- **Patents:** Pharma companies (20-year exclusivity windows), specialty chemical manufacturers
- **Regulatory licenses:** Banks (banking charter is extremely hard to obtain), utilities, spectrum holders (telecom), government contractors with security clearances
- **Data moats:** Google Search (search data improves algorithm), Meta (behavior data), credit bureaus
- **How to identify:** Is there a legal, regulatory, or psychological barrier a competitor cannot buy its way past?

### 5. Efficient Scale
The addressable market is only large enough to support one or two players profitably. A new entrant would destroy the economics for everyone, so no one enters.

- **Examples:** Natural monopolies — pipelines, water utilities, railroads, local cable monopolies
- **How to identify:** If a new competitor entered, would the market be profitable enough for both? If not, the incumbent has efficient scale.
- **Strength:** Extremely durable because competition is self-defeating
- **Weakness:** Regulatory risk (antitrust, price controls); only applies in limited markets

## Moat Width Classification

| Classification | Duration | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| **Wide Moat** | 20+ years | Multiple reinforcing moat sources; pricing power; consistently high ROIC |
| **Narrow Moat** | 10+ years | Single moat source; somewhat protected but not invincible |
| **No Moat** | <5 years | Profits will be competed away; requires constant reinvention |

## Porter's Five Forces: Industry-Level Analysis

A moat analysis tells you about the company; Five Forces tells you about the industry it operates in. Even a company with a moat can be trapped in a terrible industry.

### The 5 Forces

**1. Competitive Rivalry** — How intense is competition within the industry?
- High rivalry = price wars, margin pressure (airlines, telecom, retail)
- Low rivalry = pricing power, stable margins (luxury goods, industrial gases)

**2. Threat of New Entrants** — How easy is it for a new competitor to enter?
- High barriers = capital requirements, regulations, brand loyalty, network effects
- Low barriers = easy entry destroys margins (restaurants, content creation)

**3. Threat of Substitutes** — Can customers solve the same problem differently?
- Example: trains vs. cars vs. planes (substitute each other)
- Strong substitutes cap pricing power

**4. Bargaining Power of Suppliers** — Do input providers have leverage?
- High supplier power = inputs are scarce or dominated by few firms (chip foundries, skilled labor)
- Low supplier power = many interchangeable suppliers = company controls costs

**5. Bargaining Power of Buyers** — Do customers have leverage to push prices down?
- High buyer power = commodity product, many alternatives (auto parts, generic chemicals)
- Low buyer power = customers need it and can't negotiate (regulated utilities, patented drugs)

**Best industry characteristics:** Low rivalry + high barriers to entry + no substitutes + fragmented suppliers + loyal/captive buyers = excellent long-term economics.

**Worst industries (structurally):** Airlines (high rivalry, commodity product, high supplier power from Boeing/GE, high buyer price sensitivity, substitutes exist).

## Financial Signals of a Real Moat

| Metric | Moat Signal |
|---|---|
| ROIC > 15% for 5+ years | Moat is real and durable |
| Gross margin > industry average | Pricing power (intangibles or switching costs) |
| Revenue growth > market growth | Gaining share — not just growing with tide |
| FCF conversion > 90% | Efficient capital use |
| Stable or expanding margins over cycle | Business not commoditizing |
| Revenue grows without proportional cost growth | Operating leverage + scale advantage |

## Common Mistakes in Moat Analysis

1. **Confusing size with moat** — being big is not a moat; Walmart is big AND has a cost moat; being big alone doesn't protect profits
2. **Confusing current profitability with moat** — a company can be highly profitable today because of temporary luck (high commodity prices, post-COVID demand surge); only sustained profitability indicates a moat
3. **Not checking for moat erosion** — even wide moats can be eroded by technology disruption; Kodak had a real moat; digital photography destroyed it in 10 years
4. **Missing the industry context** — a company can have great management and still fail in a structurally bad industry (most airlines)
