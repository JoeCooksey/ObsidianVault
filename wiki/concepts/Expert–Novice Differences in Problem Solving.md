# Expert–Novice Differences in Problem Solving

The cognitive-science finding that anchors everything else: **experts and novices differ less in raw reasoning power than in how their knowledge is organized.** (Source: [[Maries & Singh 2023 — Physics Problem-Solving Review]])

## The classic studies

- **Chase & Simon (1973), de Groot (1946)** — chess masters reconstruct *real* board positions far better than novices, but lose that edge on *random* positions. Their advantage is **pattern recognition from ~50,000 stored chunks**, not better memory or logic. (Source: [[Neelen & Kirschner 2018 — Domain-Specific Knowledge 1, Generic Skills 0]])
- **Chi, Feltovich & Glaser (1981)** — physics experts sort problems by **deep principle** (conservation of energy); novices sort by **surface features** (inclines, springs).
- **Larkin et al. (1980)** — experts work *forward* from givens; novices work *backwards* via **means–end analysis**, a weak general strategy that floods working memory.

## What separates them

| | Novice | Expert |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge | isolated facts | organized **schemas** |
| Sees a problem as | surface features | deep structure |
| Working memory | overloaded | freed by chunking |
| Strategy | trial-and-error / means–end | recognize → retrieve → execute |
| Time spent | jumps to calculating | analyzes & plans first |

## The implication for getting better

You cannot become a better problem solver in the abstract. You become one by **building schemas in a domain** — through solving many problems, studying [[Worked Examples and Productive Failure|worked examples]], and the [[Deliberate Practice|deliberate practice]] of categorizing problems by deep structure. Expertise is *accumulated organized knowledge*, which is why it largely doesn't transfer across domains ([[Near vs Far Transfer]]).

> [!tip] One-line rule
> Experts don't think harder — they recognize. Build the patterns by doing many problems and reflecting on their deep structure.
