# Smoke Point (Cooking Oils)

The **smoke point** is the temperature at which a heated oil starts to visibly smoke and break down, producing acrid fumes and off-flavors. It is widely used to pick a cooking oil — but the science says it is a **weak indicator** of how an oil actually performs and degrades when heated.

## What raises or lowers a smoke point
A 2025 analytical study of 48 virgin olive oils found the dominant driver is **free fatty acid (FFA) content**: higher acidity → strongly lower smoke point (the relationship was a strong inverse correlation). Secondary factors: more **saturated fat raises** smoke point; more **oxidation products lower** it. (Source: [[Foods 2025 — Smoke Point Predictive Models for Virgin Olive Oils]])

This is why **refining raises smoke point** — it strips out the free fatty acids and impurities that smoke first. Refined avocado oil hits 480–520°F; unrefined versions of the same oils are far lower. (Source: [[Cooking Oils — Master Guide]])

## Why it's a misleading guide to "healthiness"
> [!tip] **An oil's smoke point does not correlate with its stability or its production of harmful compounds when heated.** (Source: [[De Alzaa 2018 — Chemical and Physical Changes in Oils During Heating]])

The classic example: extra-virgin olive oil has a modest smoke point (~320–375°F) yet is **more** oxidatively stable than many high-smoke-point oils, because stability is set by **fat composition and antioxidants**, not smoke temperature. (Source: [[Oxidative Stability of Cooking Oils]])

## How to actually use it
- Smoke point still matters for **flavor and not setting off the smoke alarm** — don't deep-fry in a low-smoke-point oil.
- But for *which oil is safest/healthiest under heat*, judge by **oxidative stability** (low PUFA + antioxidants), not smoke point alone.
- **Practical floor:** an oil that is actively smoking is degrading — turn down the heat or discard it.
