# The Feynman Technique

**Learn by teaching it in plain language.** Named after physicist Richard Feynman, this is the fastest way to find — and then fix — the holes in your understanding. The core claim: *if you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it.*

## The four steps

1. **Pick one concept.** Not a chapter — one idea (e.g., "Thévenin's theorem," "supply and demand," "backpropagation"). Write it at the top of a blank page.
2. **Explain it simply, as if teaching a 12-year-old.** Write out the whole explanation in plain words, no jargon. (Feynman's "rubber duck" version: explain aloud to an imaginary student.)
3. **Identify the gaps.** Wherever you stall, hand-wave, or fall back on jargon you can't unpack — that's a gap. Go back to the source material *only* for those spots.
4. **Organize, simplify, and use an analogy.** Refine the explanation into a clean story; find an everyday analogy. A good analogy proves you grasp the underlying structure, not just the words.

## Why it works

- Step 2 is **[[Active Recall (Retrieval Practice)|active recall]] plus production** — you must generate the explanation from memory, which both strengthens and *tests* the memory.
- Step 3 is **calibrated gap detection** — it exposes the difference between *recognizing* an idea (it looked familiar in the book) and *understanding* it (you can reconstruct it). This is the trap that re-reading hides.
- Using your **own words** is elaboration — connecting new material to what you already know, the mechanism with "no known limit to how much you can learn." (Source: [[Make It Stick (Brown, Roediger, McDaniel)]])

## When to use it

- After reading a section, before moving on — a fast comprehension check.
- On any concept you "sort of" get but couldn't teach.
- To compress a hard topic into notes ([[Reading Retention Methods]] / Zettelkasten-style permanent notes).
- Before an exam, to find which topics are shaky.

## Where it fits

It's the **understanding/intuition** tool — the bridge between rote memory and real comprehension. Pair it with the [[Active Recall (Retrieval Practice)|recall]] + [[Spaced Repetition|spacing]] + [[Interleaving (Learning)|interleaving]] stack to retain what you've now genuinely understood. (Source: [[Research: How to Learn Anything (The Science of Learning)]])

> [!tip] One-line rule
> The moment you get stuck explaining it in plain words is the exact moment you found what to study next.
