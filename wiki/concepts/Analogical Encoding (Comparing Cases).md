# Analogical Encoding (Comparing Cases)

One of the few techniques that reliably builds **transferable** problem-solving ability: **actively comparing two or more examples that share a deep structure** to extract the common schema.

## The core finding

**Gick & Holyoak (1983):** people given *two* analogous stories with the same causal structure could generalize that structure and transfer it to a new problem — while people given only *one* story usually couldn't, even when the solution was sitting in front of them. The act of **comparison** is what surfaces the deep structure. (Source: Gick & Holyoak 1983, via [[Teaching Discipline-Based Problem Solving (CBE-LSE 2022)]])

**Gentner, Loewenstein & Thompson (2003):** asking learners to compare cases ("what do these two have in common?") produces far better later retrieval and transfer than studying the same cases separately. Multiple examples don't increase confusion — they let you draw on more prior knowledge and separate deep structure from surface noise.

## Why it works

A single example gets encoded with all its surface details ("the pulley problem"). Comparing two examples that differ on the surface but share structure forces your mind to **strip away surface features and keep the principle** — exactly the schema that defines [[Expert–Novice Differences in Problem Solving|expertise]]. This is the mechanism behind "contrasting cases" instruction.

## How to use it

- When studying [[Worked Examples and Productive Failure|worked examples]], **place two side by side** and ask what's the same underneath.
- After solving a problem, find a *structurally similar* one that looks different and map the correspondence.
- Explicitly name the schema ("both are conservation problems," "both are dynamic-programming").

> [!tip] One-line rule
> Never study one example — study two and ask "what's the same underneath?" Comparison is what makes knowledge portable.
