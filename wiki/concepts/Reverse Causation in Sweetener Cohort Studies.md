# Reverse Causation in Sweetener Cohort Studies

The specific bias that makes almost every "diet soda linked to disease X" observational study hard to trust — and the reason regulators (JECFA) call the cancer epidemiology "not convincing."

## The Mechanism
People don't start drinking diet soda at random. The ones who do are **disproportionately already**: overweight, dieting, pre-diabetic, hypertensive, or advised by a doctor to cut sugar. So when a cohort finds "diet-soda drinkers get more diabetes," the arrow may run **backwards**: the metabolic risk came *first* and *caused* the diet-drink choice. This is **confounding by indication / reverse causation.**

## The Evidence For It
- [[Sun 2024 - Artificial Sweeteners and CVD (UK Biobank)|Sun 2024]]: **~70% of the sweetener→CVD association was mediated by pre-existing type 2 diabetes** — a direct fingerprint of this bias.
- Cohorts adjust for baseline BMI/diabetes, but **residual confounding** (severity, trajectory, lifestyle) always remains.
- JECFA 2023 explicitly cited "reverse causality, chance, bias and confounding" as reasons the aspartame–cancer epidemiology is unconvincing.

## Why It Can't Be Fully Fixed
Randomized trials solve it (random assignment breaks the link between health status and sweetener use) — but long-term RCTs with hard disease endpoints are expensive and largely don't exist. So we're stuck comparing biased long-term cohorts against clean-but-short RCTs. See [[RCT vs Cohort Evidence Split (Nonsugar Sweeteners)]].

## The Practical Rule
Treat any observational "sweetener causes X" claim as **hypothesis-generating, not settled** — especially when the absolute risk difference is small (as in [[Debras 2022 - Artificial Sweeteners and Cardiovascular Disease (NutriNet-Sante)|Debras 2022]]).
