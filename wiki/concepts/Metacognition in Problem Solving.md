# Metacognition in Problem Solving

**Thinking about your own thinking while you solve** — accurately judging where you are, choosing strategies to move forward, and reflecting afterward to learn. It is one of the three pillars of problem-solving expertise. (Source: [[Maries & Singh 2023 — Physics Problem-Solving Review]])

## What experts do that novices don't

Experts spend significant effort **analyzing and representing the problem before solving** — then monitor progress, check the answer, and reflect on what they learned. Novices jump straight to calculating. The gap is largely a **metacognitive** one, and it maps directly onto [[Pólya's Problem-Solving Method|Pólya's]] "understand" and "look back" steps.

## The evidence

- Students with **high metacognitive skill solve problems more successfully**.
- A meta-analysis spanning childhood to adulthood found metacognition **predicts academic performance even after controlling for intelligence**.
- **Metacognitive instruction must be explicit** — beginners need to be deliberately prompted to plan, monitor, and reflect; it rarely develops on its own.

## How to build it

1. **Plan out loud / on paper** before solving — state the goal, the givens, and your chosen approach.
2. **Monitor:** pause mid-solution and ask "is this working? am I closer?"
3. **Post-mortem every problem:** what was the key move? where did I get stuck and why? what would I recognize next time? (the "look back" step — most people skip it and lose the learning).
4. **Self-explain** the worked solution in your own words ([[The Feynman Technique]]); self-explanation is associated with markedly better problem-solving (Chi 1994).

> [!tip] One-line rule
> The skill isn't only solving — it's *watching yourself* solve. Plan before, monitor during, and run a post-mortem after every problem.
