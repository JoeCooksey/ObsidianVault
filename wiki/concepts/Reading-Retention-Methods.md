# Reading Retention Methods

How to dramatically increase how much you remember from books and articles. The average reader retains only 10–20% of what they read a week later; these techniques push that to 50–80%.

## The Core Problem

Most people read passively — eyes move, pages turn, nothing sticks. Passive reading is not learning. The brain encodes deeply through retrieval, not exposure. Reading twice is far less effective than reading once and then actively recalling.

## Tier 1 — High-Impact Techniques (Use Every Time)

### 1. Active Recall
After each chapter or section, **close the book and write down everything you remember**. No peeking. This forces your brain to reconstruct the information from scratch, which strengthens the neural pathway far more than re-reading.

- Studies show this **doubles retention** compared to re-reading
- Doesn't have to be perfect — gaps reveal what to re-read
- Takes 5–10 minutes per chapter, worth more than reading the chapter twice

### 2. Spaced Repetition
Review the same material at increasing time intervals: 1 day → 3 days → 7 days → 21 days → 60 days. Each review "resets" the forgetting curve.

- **Readwise** (readwise.io) automates this for book highlights — it resurfaces your highlights on a daily schedule
- Physical option: index cards or a "tickler file" with sections for each interval
- You remember **50% more** than massed review (cramming) using spaced repetition
- The right time to review is just before you would forget — that feeling of mild effort is the signal

### 3. The Feynman Technique
After reading a concept, explain it **out loud or in writing as if teaching a 12-year-old**. Where you stumble, you've found your gap.

- Step 1: Write the concept name at the top of a blank page
- Step 2: Explain it in simple language, no jargon
- Step 3: Identify gaps (where you can't explain cleanly)
- Step 4: Return to the source to fill gaps, then re-explain
- Best for technical, conceptual content — especially EE theory, math, finance principles

## Tier 2 — System Techniques (Use for Important Books)

### 4. SQ3R Method
A structured active reading framework: **Survey → Question → Read → Recite → Review**

1. **Survey**: skim chapter headers, bold text, summary — 5 minutes total; builds schema before reading
2. **Question**: convert each heading into a question ("What is X?" "Why does Y happen?")
3. **Read**: read to answer your questions
4. **Recite**: after each section, answer your questions from memory without looking
5. **Review**: after the chapter, summarize the answers and review against the text

- Most useful for non-fiction and technical books
- Transforms reading from "absorbing" to "answering"

### 5. Mortimer Adler's 4 Levels of Reading
From *How to Read a Book* (Adler & Van Doren, 1972). Not all books deserve the same level.

| Level | Name | Description |
|-------|------|-------------|
| 1 | Elementary | Basic decoding; what the words mean |
| 2 | Inspectional | Strategic skimming; understand structure in 30–60 min before deep reading |
| 3 | Analytical | Deep, systematic reading; ask 4 key questions of the author |
| 4 | Syntopical | Compare across multiple books on the same topic |

**Adler's 4 questions for every analytical read:**
1. What is the book about as a whole?
2. What is being said in detail, and how?
3. Is the book true in whole or part?
4. What of it? (So what does this mean for me?)

**Rule**: Most books deserve Level 2 (inspectional skim). Only 3–4 books per year deserve Level 3 (analytical). Level 4 is for mastering a domain.

### 6. Annotation / Marginalia
Mark the text while you read — but mark for *ideas* not for beauty.

- **Underline**: main arguments and evidence you want to keep
- **Circle**: key terms and definitions
- **Box**: names, dates, statistics
- **Star**: most important passages (limit to ≤5 per chapter)
- **Question marks**: where you disagree or need more info
- **Arrows**: connections to earlier content

The goal is to make the book a conversation, not a highlight reel. Your marginal notes should be thoughts, not just underlining.

### 7. Progressive Summarization (Tiago Forte)
A 3-pass layered approach for notes:

- **Pass 1**: Read and highlight anything worth revisiting (10–20% of text)
- **Pass 2**: Bold the most important highlights (20–30% of your highlights)
- **Pass 3**: Write a summary in your own words at the top — this is the value

At any future point you can read: just summary → just bold highlights → all highlights → full notes. The effort is front-loaded but future retrieval is instant.

## Tier 3 — Supporting Techniques

### 8. Teach or Discuss
Explaining a book to another person is the ultimate retention test.

- Book clubs, study groups, or even telling a friend the main ideas at dinner
- The *generation effect*: producing information (explaining) beats consuming it (re-reading) for long-term retention
- Mastermind groups get "far more value from discussions than from the information alone"

### 9. Sleep Immediately After Important Reading
Neuroscience finding: sleep within hours of learning consolidates memory via hippocampal replay. Reading a key book before bed (not doom-scrolling) maximizes overnight consolidation.

### 10. Write a One-Page Summary
After finishing a book, write a single page: main argument, 3–5 key ideas, and one action you're taking. This forces synthesis, not just recall.

## What Doesn't Work

| Technique | Reality |
|-----------|---------|
| **Re-reading** | Illusion of fluency; doesn't strengthen retrieval |
| **Highlighting only** | Passive; no retrieval = no retention |
| **Reading speed courses** | Speed/comprehension tradeoff; rushing reduces retention |
| **Reading multiple books simultaneously** | Fragments context; serial reading is better for retention |

## Practical Protocol for Joe

**For every important book:**
1. **Before**: Skim table of contents and chapter summaries (inspectional read, 15 min)
2. **During**: Annotate in the margins; star top ideas (≤5/chapter)
3. **After each chapter**: Active recall — close book, write what you remember (5 min)
4. **After the book**: Write one-page summary; enter best highlights into Readwise
5. **Day 3, Week 2, Month 1**: Readwise resurfaces highlights automatically

**Time cost**: adds ~25% to reading time; multiplies retention 3–5×.
