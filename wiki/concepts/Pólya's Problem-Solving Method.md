# Pólya's Problem-Solving Method

A reusable **process** for attacking any problem, from [[George Polya|George Pólya's]] *How to Solve It* (1945). Not an algorithm that guarantees an answer — a set of guiding questions ([[heuristics]]) that raise the odds of finding one. (Source: [[Polya 1945 — How to Solve It]])

## The four steps

1. **Understand the problem.** What is the unknown? What are the data? What is the condition? Restate it in your own words; draw a figure; introduce notation. *Most failed solutions skip this step.*
2. **Devise a plan.** Find the connection between data and unknown. Reach for a heuristic (below). If stuck, find a related/simpler problem you *can* solve.
3. **Carry out the plan.** Execute step by step, checking each move is correct.
4. **Look back.** Verify the result; re-derive it differently if possible; ask **"can I use this result or method on another problem?"** — this is the step that turns one solved problem into a transferable [[Expert–Novice Differences in Problem Solving|schema]].

## The core heuristics

- **Work backwards** from the goal.
- **Solve a simpler/related problem** first, then generalize.
- **Specialize** (try a concrete case) or **generalize** (solve the family).
- **Analogy** — find a problem with the same structure (see [[Analogical Encoding (Comparing Cases)]]).
- **Decompose and recombine** — break into sub-problems.
- **Introduce auxiliary elements** — a diagram, an extra line, new notation, an intermediate goal.

## Why it works

The steps are an externalized form of **[[Metacognition in Problem Solving|metacognition]]** — they force the planning and self-monitoring that experts do automatically and novices skip. The method is a *scaffold*, not a substitute for domain knowledge: heuristics only fire when you have schemas to apply them to ([[Domain-Specific Knowledge vs General Problem-Solving Skill]]).

> [!tip] One-line rule
> Spend most of your effort *understanding the problem and looking back* — the two steps everyone rushes past.
