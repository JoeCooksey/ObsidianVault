# Position Sizing and Risk Management

> The factor that decides survival. *What* you buy matters less than *how much* you risk and *whether you live to trade again.* Most blow-ups are sizing failures, not stock-picking failures.

## The Central Idea

Position sizing answers **"how much capital does this idea get?"** — separately from "is this a good idea?" A great thesis sized too large can bankrupt you on a normal drawdown; a mediocre thesis sized small cannot. Risk management is the discipline that keeps any single mistake non-fatal.

## Core Rules

1. **Risk 1–2% of total capital per trade.** Risk = (entry − stop) × shares. With a $95 stop on a $100 stock, risk/share is $5; a $500 risk budget → 100 shares max. (Source: Lime, QuantifiedStrategies) (confidence: high)
2. **Set a stop-loss before entry**, not after the price falls — a pre-committed exit removes emotion in the moment.
3. **Cap daily/weekly loss limits** to prevent revenge trading after a bad run.
4. **Diversify across assets, sectors, and geographies** — spreading exposure reduces dependence on any one bet.
5. **Risk-based (not equal-dollar) sizing** — allocate more to low-volatility, uncorrelated positions and less to high-volatility, correlated ones. (confidence: medium)

## Why It Dominates Returns

A 50% loss requires a 100% gain to recover. Avoiding large drawdowns compounds better than chasing large gains. This is the quantitative core of [[Margin of Safety (Finance)]] applied to a *portfolio* rather than a single security. Even Renaissance keeps Medallion small ($10B cap) so its strategy doesn't move the market against itself — sizing discipline at the firm level (see [[Renaissance Technologies]]).

## The Kelly Criterion (advanced)

Ed Thorp brought the **Kelly criterion** from blackjack to markets: bet a fraction of capital proportional to your edge divided by the odds. Full Kelly maximizes long-run growth but is brutally volatile; most practitioners use "fractional Kelly" (½ or less). (confidence: medium) See [[Ed Thorp]].

## For a Beginner

The simplest risk management is structural: **own the whole market via index funds and dollar-cost average** ([[Index Fund Investing]]). It makes single-stock blow-ups impossible by construction — no position sizing decision required.
