# Financial Statement Analysis for Investors

Every publicly traded company in the US files three core financial statements with the SEC. Together, they tell you whether a company is profitable, financially stable, and generating real cash.

**Rule:** No single statement tells the full story. A company can show profits while bleeding cash, or clean income while hiding problems on the balance sheet. Read all three — together.

**Where to find them:** SEC EDGAR (sec.gov) → search by company name → 10-K (annual, audited) or 10-Q (quarterly, unaudited)

---

## Statement 1: Income Statement

**What it answers:** Is the company profitable? Is it growing?

Also called the "P&L" (Profit & Loss). Shows revenue and expenses over a period (quarter or year).

### Line Items (Top to Bottom)

| Line | What It Is |
|---|---|
| **Revenue** | Total sales; the top line |
| **Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)** | Direct costs to produce the product/service |
| **Gross Profit** | Revenue − COGS |
| **Gross Margin %** | Gross Profit / Revenue — pricing power signal |
| **Operating Expenses (OpEx)** | R&D, Sales & Marketing, G&A (overhead) |
| **EBIT / Operating Income** | Gross Profit − OpEx — core business profit |
| **Operating Margin %** | EBIT / Revenue — operational efficiency |
| **Interest Expense** | Cost of debt |
| **EBT (Earnings Before Tax)** | EBIT − Interest |
| **Net Income** | After-tax profit — the "bottom line" |
| **EPS (Earnings Per Share)** | Net Income / Diluted Shares Outstanding |

### What to Look For
- **Revenue growth rate** — is it accelerating, stable, or declining?
- **Gross margin trend** — expanding = pricing power; contracting = competitive pressure
- **Operating leverage** — does revenue grow faster than expenses? (good sign)
- **EPS growth** — but watch for EPS growth via share buybacks masking flat net income
- **R&D as % of revenue** — tech/pharma need high R&D to stay competitive

### Red Flags
- Revenue growth but shrinking margins (losing pricing power)
- Net income growing but operating income flat (financial engineering, not real profit)
- One-time gains inflating net income in a specific quarter (always check "non-recurring items")

## Statement 2: Balance Sheet

**What it answers:** Is the company financially stable? What does it own and owe?

A snapshot at a single point in time (quarter-end or year-end). Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity — always.

### Structure

**ASSETS (what the company owns)**

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Current Assets** | Cash, accounts receivable, inventory (convertible to cash within 1 yr) |
| **Non-Current Assets** | PP&E (buildings, equipment), intangibles (patents, brand), goodwill |
| **Total Assets** | Everything the company owns |

**LIABILITIES (what the company owes)**

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Current Liabilities** | Accounts payable, short-term debt, accrued expenses (due within 1 yr) |
| **Long-Term Liabilities** | Long-term debt, pension obligations, deferred revenue |
| **Total Liabilities** | Everything owed to others |

**SHAREHOLDERS' EQUITY**
- Total Assets − Total Liabilities
- Retained earnings (accumulated profits not paid as dividends)
- Book value per share = Equity / Shares Outstanding

### Key Balance Sheet Ratios

| Ratio | Formula | Healthy |
|---|---|---|
| **Current Ratio** | Current Assets / Current Liabilities | ≥ 1.5 |
| **Quick Ratio** | (Cash + Receivables) / Current Liabilities | ≥ 1.0 |
| **D/E Ratio** | Total Debt / Shareholders' Equity | <1.0 for most industries |
| **Net Debt** | Total Debt − Cash | Negative = net cash position (excellent) |
| **Debt/EBITDA** | Total Debt / EBITDA | <3× is manageable |

### What to Look For
- **Net cash position** — company has more cash than debt (fortress balance sheet)
- **Goodwill** — represents premiums paid on acquisitions; high goodwill means past acquisition-driven growth; watch for goodwill impairment charges
- **Accounts receivable growth vs. revenue growth** — if receivables grow faster than revenue, customers aren't paying (revenue recognition issue)
- **Inventory trends** — rising inventory without rising sales = products not moving

### Red Flags
- Debt/EBITDA > 5× (over-leveraged; vulnerable in recession)
- Negative book value (liabilities exceed assets — often in PE buyouts, but concerning for organics)
- Rapid growth in "other assets" or unusual items on the balance sheet
- Current ratio < 1.0 (can't cover short-term obligations)

## Statement 3: Cash Flow Statement

**What it answers:** Is the company generating real cash? Is reported profit backed by cash?

This is the hardest statement to manipulate — cash is cash. Divided into three sections.

### Three Sections

**Section 1: Operating Cash Flow (OCF)**
Cash generated from core business operations. Start with net income and adjust for non-cash items (depreciation, stock comp) and working capital changes.
- **OCF should be positive and growing** for a healthy company
- OCF should roughly track net income over time
- Large, persistent gap between net income and OCF = accounting manipulation risk

**Section 2: Investing Cash Flow**
Cash used for/received from investing activities.
- **Capex** (Capital Expenditures): spending on PP&E — required to maintain and grow the business
- Asset sales, acquisitions, investments
- High capex means capital-intensive business (manufacturing, telecom, utilities)

**Section 3: Financing Cash Flow**
Cash flows related to debt and equity.
- Debt issuance vs. repayment
- Dividends paid
- Share buybacks vs. share issuance (issuance dilutes existing shareholders)

### The #1 Metric: Free Cash Flow (FCF)

**FCF = Operating Cash Flow − Capital Expenditures**

This is what the company can actually return to shareholders after maintaining the business.

| FCF Metric | Formula | What It Tells You |
|---|---|---|
| **Free Cash Flow** | OCF − Capex | Real cash available to shareholders |
| **FCF Margin** | FCF / Revenue | % of every dollar of revenue that becomes cash |
| **FCF Yield** | FCF / Market Cap | Cash return if you owned the whole company; >5% is attractive |
| **FCF Conversion** | FCF / Net Income | Should be close to 100%; <70% consistently = concern |

### What to Look For
- OCF > Net Income over time (depreciation adds back, conservative accounting)
- FCF growing consistently over 3–5 years
- FCF yield above 5% (indicates undervaluation or strong cash generation)
- Low capex relative to OCF = "asset-light" business model (SaaS, financial services) — often better economics

### Red Flags
- Net income positive but OCF negative (watch: working capital build, revenue recognition)
- FCF significantly below net income for multiple years in a row
- High capex with no corresponding revenue growth (capital allocation problem)
- Large share issuance — company is diluting you to fund itself

## Reading All Three Together: The Full Picture

| Scenario | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Profits ↑, FCF ↓ | Check receivables, inventory — may be fake revenue |
| Profits flat, FCF ↑ | Improving working capital efficiency — often bullish |
| High debt + low OCF | Danger: may struggle to service debt |
| Revenue ↑, margins ↓ | Growing into a competitive market — pricing power eroding |
| Net cash + FCF ↑ | Fortress balance sheet — low risk, strong business |
