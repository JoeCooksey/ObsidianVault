# Active Recall (Retrieval Practice)

**The single highest-utility learning technique in the evidence base.** You learn by *pulling information out* of your head, not by *putting it in* again. Every time you successfully retrieve a memory, you strengthen it and make it easier to retrieve next time.

## The core idea

> [!quote]
> "Testing isn't simply a way of assessing knowledge but a way of creating it." — *Ultralearning*

The brain treats retrieval as a signal that a memory is worth keeping. Re-reading and highlighting feel productive because they create *fluency* (the material looks familiar), but familiarity is not the same as the ability to produce the answer cold. (Source: [[Make It Stick (Brown, Roediger, McDaniel)]])

## Why it beats re-reading

- **Practice testing** is rated **high utility** — it works across ages, ability levels, and subjects — in the Dunlosky meta-analysis. (Source: [[Dunlosky 2013 — Improving Students' Learning]])
- Re-reading and highlighting are rated **low utility** despite being the most popular student strategies. (Source: [[Dunlosky 2013 — Improving Students' Learning]])
- Retrieval is a [[Desirable Difficulties|desirable difficulty]]: it feels harder than re-reading, and that effortful struggle is exactly what produces durable memory.

## How to do it

1. **Close the book and answer from memory.** After reading/watching, write down everything you remember before checking.
2. **Use questions, not summaries.** Turn headings and facts into questions; answer them blind.
3. **Test before you feel ready.** Retrieve *before* you're confident — the struggle is the mechanism, not a sign you studied wrong.
4. **Always get feedback.** Check your answer against the source so errors don't get encoded. Feedback sharply raises the effect. (Source: [[Dunlosky 2013 — Improving Students' Learning]])
5. **Free recall → cued recall → recognition.** Hardest (blank-page brain dump) is most powerful; multiple-choice is weakest.

## Practical tools

- Flashcards (paper or [[Spaced Repetition|Anki]]) — the canonical retrieval tool.
- The blank-page "brain dump" after a study session or lecture.
- [[The Feynman Technique]] — explaining a concept from memory is retrieval *plus* gap-detection.
- Past exams / problem sets done closed-book.

## Where it fits

Active recall is the **retrieval** pillar that the whole science of learning converges on. Combine it with [[Spaced Repetition]] (recall at increasing intervals) and [[Interleaving (Learning)]] (mix what you recall) for the strongest evidence-based study stack. (Source: [[Research: How to Learn Anything (The Science of Learning)]])

> [!tip] One-line rule
> If a study activity doesn't make you *produce* the answer, it probably isn't learning — it's reviewing.
