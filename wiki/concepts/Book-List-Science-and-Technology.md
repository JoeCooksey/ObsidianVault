# Book List — Science and Technology

Books that build the mental model of a scientist and engineer. Covers the history of ideas, the practice of technical excellence, and the biographies of the people who built the modern world.

## S-Tier

### Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman! — Richard Feynman (1985)
The collected stories of Richard Feynman — physicist, bongo player, safecracker, artist, Nobel laureate. Not a physics book, but a book about **how a curious, playful mind engages with the world**.

- The most entertaining science book ever written
- Core lesson: the goal is understanding, not performance; genuine curiosity beats credential-chasing
- Feynman's problem-solving approach (break everything down to first principles, play with ideas, never bluff) is the best engineering mindset book ever written without trying to be
- **Joe relevance**: Feynman was an EE-adjacent physicist obsessed with how things work at the fundamental level — exact same mindset needed for WBG power electronics

### The Idea Factory: Bell Labs and the Great Age of American Innovation — Jon Gertner (2012)
The history of Bell Labs (1920s–1980s) — the most productive research institution in history. Invented: the transistor, the laser, information theory, UNIX, C, cellular networks, solar cells, and more. All from one building in New Jersey.

- Core lesson: **how great innovation actually happens** — slow, collaborative, patient, rigorous
- Characters: Claude Shannon, William Shockley, John Pierce, Bill Baker, Ken Thompson
- Directly relevant to understanding what Sandia/LLNL/DARPA labs are like
- **Joe relevance**: Bell Labs is the template for national lab culture; understanding it is understanding LLNL/Sandia

### The Art of Doing Science and Engineering — Richard Hamming (1997)
Hamming (Bell Labs mathematician, invented error-correcting codes) gave a lecture series in his 70s distilling everything he learned about becoming technically excellent. This is the content of those lectures.

- The "Great Researchers" chapter alone is worth the price of the book
- Core lesson: what separates excellent researchers from good ones is working on important problems, not just hard ones
- His question: "What are the most important problems in your field, and why aren't you working on them?"
- **Joe relevance**: direct guide to how to aim your EE career for high impact rather than adequate output

## A-Tier

### The Innovators — Walter Isaacson (2014)
History of the digital revolution from Ada Lovelace (1840s) to the internet (2000s). Covers Babbage, Turing, von Neumann, Shockley, Shannon, Noyce, Jobs, Gates, Berners-Lee.

- Best narrative context for understanding where computers came from
- Core lesson: the most impactful innovations came from teams, not lone geniuses; collaboration matters
- **Joe relevance**: gives EE students a genealogy; understanding Shockley → Fairchild → Intel is the Silicon Valley origin story

### A Mind at Play: How Claude Shannon Invented the Information Age — Soni & Goodman (2017)
Biography of Claude Shannon — the mathematician who single-handedly created information theory (1948), the foundation of all digital communication, data storage, and coding theory.

- Shannon was one of the most playful, unconventional minds in science — rode a unicycle in Bell Labs hallways while juggling
- His 1948 paper is the most impactful single scientific paper of the 20th century
- **Joe relevance**: digital communications, error correction, and data compression are all downstream of Shannon; EE students encounter his work in signals and communications courses

### The Code Book — Simon Singh (1999)
History of cryptography from Caesar cipher to RSA. Covers: substitution ciphers, the Vigenère cipher, Enigma/WWII, public-key encryption, quantum cryptography.

- Among the best science narrative books — written for non-mathematicians but covers real math
- Core lesson: the history of communication security is an arms race between mathematicians
- **Joe relevance**: RSA and AES are used in every secure digital system; understanding why matters for embedded systems and security work

### Gödel, Escher, Bach: An Eternal Golden Braid — Douglas Hofstadter (1979, Pulitzer Prize)
About self-reference, recursion, formal systems, consciousness, and the limits of mathematical reasoning. Uses Bach fugues and Escher drawings as recurring themes.

- The most difficult book on this list — read one chapter per week
- Core lesson: the deepest ideas in math (Gödel's incompleteness theorem), music, and visual art all share the same fundamental structure
- **Joe relevance**: computer science, digital logic, and formal verification all connect to these ideas; this is the book that gave many great engineers their deepest intuitions

## B-Tier

### The Making of the Atomic Bomb — Richard Rhodes (1986, Pulitzer Prize)
The complete story of the Manhattan Project — physics, engineering, organizational challenges, and moral weight.

- Densest and most complete account; not light reading
- Core lesson: how science and engineering scale under wartime urgency; what happens when theoretical physics becomes engineering reality
- **Joe relevance**: LLNL/Sandia are direct institutional descendants of the Manhattan Project; this is the founding mythology of the national labs

### The Double Helix — James Watson (1968)
Watson's first-person account of discovering DNA's structure. Controversial (Watson's treatment of Rosalind Franklin) but important.

- Core lesson: how scientific competition actually works; the messiness of discovery
- **Joe relevance**: biology isn't EE, but the sociology of competitive science research is directly applicable to research labs

## Reading Order for Joe
1. *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!* — read this first; sets the mindset
2. *The Art of Doing Science and Engineering* (Hamming) — read early in ASU EE; shapes how to aim
3. *A Mind at Play* (Shannon) — during signals & systems coursework
4. *The Idea Factory* (Bell Labs) — before applying to LLNL/Sandia (context for lab culture)
5. *The Innovators* — any time; great Zone 2 audiobook
6. *The Code Book* — when taking digital systems or cybersecurity
7. *GEB* — one chapter per week, ongoing throughout college
