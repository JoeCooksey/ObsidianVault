# Spanish Pronunciation Guide

Spanish pronunciation is highly regular — once you know the rules, every word is spelled exactly as it sounds. The problem for English speakers is not complexity but *English habits* that must be overridden. Fix pronunciation early; bad habits become permanent.

---

## The Key Advantage of Spanish Pronunciation

**Spanish is phonetically consistent.** Each letter has one sound (mostly). Once you learn the Spanish sound-to-letter mappings, you can pronounce any Spanish word you've never seen — unlike English where "though," "through," "thought," and "tough" all use the same letters to produce four different sounds.

---

## The Five Vowels — Master These First

Spanish has five pure vowel sounds. They never reduce to schwa (the "uh" sound English uses for unstressed syllables). This is the #1 English-speaker mistake.

| Letter | Sound | English Approximation | Example |
|--------|-------|-----------------------|---------|
| A | /a/ | "ah" (never "uh") | *amigo* = "ah-MEE-go" |
| E | /e/ | "eh" (never "ay") | *mesa* = "MEH-sah" |
| I | /i/ | "ee" | *libro* = "LEE-bro" |
| O | /o/ | "oh" (never "ow") | *poco* = "POH-ko" |
| U | /u/ | "oo" | *mucho* = "MOO-cho" |

**Critical rule:** Spanish vowels are *pure monophthongs* — they don't glide like English vowels. English "oh" glides to "ow" at the end; Spanish "o" stays flat. English "ay" glides to "ee"; Spanish "e" stays flat.

**Practice drill:** Say "MESA" — if it sounds like "MAY-suh," both vowels are wrong. Should be "MEH-sah."

## Consonants — The Four Problem Areas

### Problem 1: The R Sounds (Most Important)

Spanish has two R sounds; English speakers use neither.

**Single R = tap [ɾ]:**
- Tongue tip briefly taps the roof of the mouth (alveolar ridge) once
- Similar to the "tt" or "dd" sound in American English "butter" or "ladder"
- "pero" (but) = "PEH-ɾo"
- Practice: Say "butter" fast → the "tt" is your Spanish single-R

**Double RR = trill [r]:**
- Tongue tip vibrates rapidly against the roof of the mouth
- "perro" (dog) = "PEH-rro" (trill)
- Cannot use English /ɹ/ — it sounds completely foreign to native speakers
- Practice tip: Blow air over your tongue while keeping it loose against your palate; takes weeks to develop

**Minimal pair practice:** pero (but) vs. perro (dog); caro (expensive) vs. carro (car/cart)

---

### Problem 2: The D Sound

English /d/ is a hard stop. Spanish /d/ changes depending on position:

- **At word start (after pause or n):** hard [d] like English — "donde," "dar"
- **Between vowels or after most consonants:** softens to [ð] — same sound as English "the"
  - "Madrid" = "mah-DREED" with a soft, barely-touched final D
  - "cada" (each) = "KAH-ða" — the D is barely there
  - "nada" (nothing) = "NAH-ða" — very soft

Rule of thumb: If a D has vowels on both sides, treat it like the English "th" in "the."

---

### Problem 3: B and V Are the Same

In Spanish, B and V produce the same sound. There is no teeth-on-lip /v/ in Spanish.

- **At word start or after m/n:** both B and V make a hard /b/ like English B
- **Between vowels:** both soften to /β/ — a bilabial approximant (lips barely touching, not closed)
  - "vino" (wine) = "BEE-no" — starts with hard /b/
  - "uva" (grape) = "OO-βa" — soft middle sound

Don't worry about perfecting /β/ immediately; just stop making the English /v/ sound with V-spelled words.

---

### Problem 4: J and G (Before E/I) — The Spanish H

The Spanish J and G (before e or i) make a guttural sound /x/ — like clearing your throat, or the "ch" in Scottish "loch."

- "Juan" = "HWahn" but with a rougher H
- "jamón" (ham) = "hah-MOHN" (guttural h)
- "general" = "heh-NEH-rahl" (not the English "general")
- "México" = "MEH-hee-ko" (the X here = /x/)

In practice: Many learners approximate this with a strong English H and are perfectly understood. The full /x/ comes with time and listening.

## Full Consonant Quick Reference

| Letter | Sound | Notes |
|--------|-------|-------|
| B | /b/ or /β/ | Hard at start; soft between vowels |
| C (before a,o,u) | /k/ | "casa" = KAH-sa |
| C (before e,i) | /s/ (Americas) or /θ/ (Spain) | "ciudad" = syoo-DAHD (LatAm) or thyoo-DAHD (Spain) |
| CH | /tʃ/ | Always "ch" as in "cheese" |
| D | /d/ or /ð/ | Hard at start; soft between vowels |
| G (before a,o,u) | /g/ | "gato" = GAH-to |
| G (before e,i) | /x/ | Guttural — "general," "gitano" |
| H | Silent | Never pronounced — "hola" = OHlah |
| J | /x/ | Guttural — "Juan," "jamón" |
| LL | /ʝ/ or /j/ | Like English Y — "llama" = YAH-mah |
| Ñ | /ɲ/ | Like "ny" — "mañana" = mah-NYAH-nah |
| Q | /k/ | Always paired with silent U — "que" = keh |
| R | /ɾ/ (single) / /r/ (double) | Tap vs trill |
| S | /s/ | Never buzzed like English Z |
| V | /b/ or /β/ | Same as B — no English /v/ |
| X | /ks/ usually, /x/ in some | "éxito" = EK-see-to; "México" = MEH-hee-ko |
| Y | /ʝ/ or /j/ | Like English Y — "yo" = YO |
| Z | /s/ (Americas) or /θ/ (Spain) | "zapato" = sah-PAH-to (LatAm) |

## Stress Rules

Spanish stress is predictable:

1. **Words ending in a vowel, N, or S:** stress falls on the SECOND-TO-LAST syllable
   - "casa" → CA-sa; "hablan" → HA-blan; "libros" → LI-bros

2. **Words ending in any other consonant:** stress falls on the LAST syllable
   - "ciudad" → ciu-DAD; "hablar" → ha-BLAR; "papel" → pa-PEL

3. **Written accent mark (´) overrides both rules:**
   - "café" → ca-FÉ (accent shows stress is on last syllable even though it ends in vowel)
   - "fácil" → FÁ-cil (accent shows stress on first syllable despite vowel ending)

Once you know these three rules, you can stress any Spanish word correctly.

## Spain vs. Latin American Spanish (Dialect Differences)

| Feature | Latin American | Spain (Castilian) |
|---------|---------------|-------------------|
| C before e/i | /s/ ("ciudad" = SYOO-dahd) | /θ/ (like "thin" — "thiudahd") |
| Z | /s/ | /θ/ |
| LL | /j/ (like Y) | /ʎ/ or /j/ (varies by region) |
| Vosotros | Not used | Used for informal "you all" |
| Vos | Used in Argentina/Uruguay | Not used |

**For Joe:** Since you're in Arizona, you'll be exposed primarily to **Mexican Spanish** — the dominant dialect in the US Southwest. Stick with LatAm pronunciation; you'll still understand Spaniards easily with exposure.

## Practical Pronunciation Drill Routine

**Week 1–2 (5 min/day):**
1. Read alphabet, pronouncing each letter in isolation
2. Practice five pure vowels: "ah-eh-ee-oh-oo" 20× until automatic
3. Practice minimal pairs: pero/perro, cara/carro, caro/carro

**Week 3–4:**
4. Read aloud from Language Transfer transcripts (or any beginner text)
5. Record yourself, compare to native speaker (italki tutor or Forvo.com)
6. Focus on D softening: say "nada," "cada," "todo" with soft D

**Month 2+:**
7. Shadow Dreaming Spanish videos — pause, repeat, shadow speaker exactly
8. One italki session specifically for pronunciation feedback
9. Practice trill R daily (5 min tongue exercises while brushing teeth)
