# Rational Egoism

The doctrine — popular among 1860s Russian radicals and associated with Nikolai Chernyshevsky's *What Is to Be Done?* — that acting in one's own **enlightened self-interest is rational**, and that a properly reasoned pursuit of self-interest automatically produces the greatest social good. Morality is reduced to arithmetic: weigh costs and benefits, and the "logical" choice is the ethical one.

In *[[Crime and Punishment - Fyodor Dostoevsky|Crime and Punishment]]*, Dostoevsky puts this philosophy in the mouths of unappealing characters — the utilitarian calculus [[Rodion Raskolnikov|Raskolnikov]] overhears ("kill one useless pawnbroker, use her money for a thousand good deeds"), and the smug egoism of Luzhin and Lebezyatnikov. The novel argues that human beings are not calculators: Raskolnikov's own conscience violently rejects the "logical" murder, and reason cut off from love and faith becomes a machine for self-destruction. Rational egoism is the seedbed from which [[The Extraordinary Man Theory]] grows.

*Source: [[Crime and Punishment - Fyodor Dostoevsky]].*
