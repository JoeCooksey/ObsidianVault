# Latarjet Procedure

A bone-block stabilization: the surgeon transfers the **coracoid process** (with its attached conjoint tendon) to the front of the glenoid. It stops instability three ways — it **replaces lost socket bone**, the wrapped tendon creates a **dynamic sling** across the front, and it reconstructs the capsule. Used when soft-tissue [[Arthroscopic Bankart Repair]] is likely to fail.

## When it's chosen over Bankart repair
- **Significant glenoid bone loss** (bony Bankart) or a large engaging Hill-Sachs.
- **Failed prior Bankart repair** / revision instability.
- **High-risk patients**: collision athletes, marked hyperlaxity, many prior dislocations.

## How well it works
| Outcome (mean ~13-yr) | Bankart | Latarjet |
|---|---|---|
| Recurrence | 35% | **10%** |
| Stable at 15 yrs | 64% | **90%** |
| Revision for instability | 20% | 5% |

(Source: [[Delgado et al 2025 — Bankart vs Latarjet Long-Term Follow-up]].) In adolescents the same study group found 35% vs 6% recurrence; combining a Bristow-Latarjet with Bankart in high-risk teens cut recurrence to ~8% vs 22%.

## Trade-offs
- **Lower recurrence and better durability** than Bankart in higher-risk shoulders.
- More invasive; distinct (uncommon but real) complications — hardware/screw issues, graft nonunion/resorption, nerve risk. Overall complication rates in matched studies were **similar** to Bankart (~15–17%), but the *type* of complication differs and some are more serious.
- Often reserved for the cases that need it rather than used as a default first operation.

> [!gap] Bankart-vs-Latarjet is an individualized decision based on measured bone loss, activity, and laxity — there is no single recurrence number that applies to everyone, and it requires a shoulder surgeon's assessment with CT.
