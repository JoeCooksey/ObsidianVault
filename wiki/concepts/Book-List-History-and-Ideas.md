# Book List — History and Ideas

Books that build big-picture thinking: how civilizations rise and fall, how systems work, how ideas change the world. This is the "mental model" category — the books that change how you think about everything else.

## Why This Category Matters for Engineers

Engineers often optimize locally (this circuit, this algorithm, this system) but rarely think at the civilizational scale of "why does this technology exist?" and "what does it change about how humans live?" The books in this category provide that context. They also tend to produce the most durable mental models — frameworks that apply across every domain.

## S-Tier

### Sapiens: A Brief History of Humankind — Yuval Noah Harari (2011)
The macro-history of our species from 70,000 years ago to the present. Covers the Cognitive Revolution, Agricultural Revolution, and Scientific Revolution as organizing frameworks.

- The most-gifted non-fiction book of the 2010s for good reason
- Core thesis: humans dominate Earth because we are uniquely able to cooperate around shared fictions (religions, nations, money, corporations)
- Best mental model: **imagined orders** — most of what drives human behavior is imagined (legal systems, money, organizations), not physical; this changes how you understand every institution
- **Joe relevance**: understanding corporations, universities, and national labs as "imagined orders" that coordinate large groups changes how you navigate them

### Guns, Germs, and Steel — Jared Diamond (1997, Pulitzer Prize)
Why did Eurasian civilizations conquer the Americas, Africa, and Australia rather than the reverse? Diamond's answer: geography and biology, not racial differences or superior intelligence.

- The definitive macro-history of technological inequality
- Core thesis: the orientation of continents (E-W vs N-S), availability of domesticable crops and animals, and population density drove technological divergence
- Best mental model: **proximate vs. ultimate causation** — don't stop at the immediate cause; ask why the cause exists
- **Joe relevance**: systems thinking about large-scale cause and effect; the proximate/ultimate distinction is directly useful in engineering problem analysis

### The Wright Brothers — David McCullough (2015)
Two bicycle mechanics with no college education, no wealthy backers, and no government funding built the first airplane in 1903 while government-funded projects failed. How?

- Core thesis: deliberate practice, systematic experimentation, obsessive iteration, and willingness to fail beat funding and credentials
- The best biography argument for building skills over waiting for opportunity
- **Joe relevance**: the Wright Brothers at 19 were already building things obsessively; their story is the best argument for Joe's Year 1 self-study approach

## A-Tier

### Thinking in Systems: A Primer — Donella Meadows (2008)
The clearest introduction to systems thinking: stocks, flows, feedback loops (reinforcing and balancing), delays, and leverage points.

- Meadows was a systems scientist at MIT; this was her final, most accessible book
- Core mental model: every problem is a system problem; finding leverage points > optimizing individual components
- Best tool: the **feedback loop diagram** — once you can see feedback loops, you see them everywhere (market dynamics, biological regulation, control systems)
- **Joe relevance**: control systems is literally systems thinking applied to circuits; understanding this book before Control Theory makes that course far more intuitive

### Nexus: A Brief History of Information Networks from the Stone Age to AI — Harari (2024)
Harari's follow-up to Sapiens focused on information networks — how language, writing, printing, and now AI reshape human cooperation and power structures.

- Updates Sapiens for the AI era
- Core thesis: the power to control information networks = civilizational power; this was true in 500 CE and is true in 2026
- **Joe relevance**: engineers building AI systems are building the current layer of this history; knowing the pattern matters

### The Power of Habit — Charles Duhigg (2012)
Extends the habit loop research with institutional and societal case studies (Alcoa safety culture, Starbucks training, Montgomery bus boycott as habit change).

- Pairs with [[Atomic Habits]] — Duhigg focuses on macro-scale habit change (organizations, social movements), Clear focuses on individual
- Core insight: institutional culture is a pattern of organizational habits; change the keystone habits, change the organization
- **Joe relevance**: understanding how engineering teams' cultures function (at Sandia, Tesla, Bell Labs) through a habit lens

### The Lessons of History — Will and Ariel Durant (1968)
Two historians summarize the recurring patterns of history in 100 pages. Dense, quotable, one of the best "essence" books ever written.

- Covers: biology, morality, religion, economics, government, war, progress
- Core insight: "The only lasting revolution is in the mind of man" — institutions change slowly; human nature doesn't
- Best single chapter: the economics chapter, which predicts the 2008 crisis 40 years early
- **Joe relevance**: 100-page book; should be read twice before age 30; best return-per-page ratio on this list

## B-Tier

### The Storm Before the Storm — Mike Duncan (2017)
The fall of the Roman Republic (133–43 BC), focusing on the generation before Caesar — Marius, Sulla, Cicero, Crassus. The political institutions that had held Rome for 500 years collapsed not from external threat but from internal dysfunction.

- The most relevant historical parallel to many modern institutional crises
- Core lesson: once the norms that hold institutions together begin to erode, they erode fast
- **Joe relevance**: historical pattern recognition; norms in technical institutions, companies, and research labs follow similar dynamics

### The Wright Brothers (already S-tier above) and Empire of the Summer Moon (Gwynne, 2010)
The Comanche Nation at peak power vs. the expanding American frontier — Pulitzer finalist; a different angle on how technological and organizational advantages determine outcomes in conflict.

|------|---------------------|
| Sapiens | Imagined orders; cognitive revolution |
| Guns, Germs, Steel | Proximate vs. ultimate causation; geography as constraint |
| The Wright Brothers | Deliberate practice beats funding |
| Thinking in Systems | Feedback loops; leverage points |
| The Lessons of History | Historical patterns repeat; human nature is constant |

## Reading Order for Joe
1. *Sapiens* — first; foundational macro-mental-model
2. *The Wright Brothers* — read during Year 1 self-study period; motivational + instructive
3. *Guns, Germs, and Steel* — after Sapiens; extends the framework
4. *Thinking in Systems* — before or alongside Control Systems coursework
5. *The Lessons of History* — 100 pages; read any time; extremely dense
6. *Nexus* — later; after Sapiens and some AI context built up
