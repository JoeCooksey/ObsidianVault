# Motivational Interviewing

A person-centered way of talking that helps someone **resolve their own ambivalence about change** — instead of arguing them into it. Developed by William Miller and Stephen Rollnick, originally for alcohol use. The defining move: **you do NOT try to convince.** Convincing triggers resistance; people are persuaded by hearing *themselves* argue for change.

> [!key] The "righting reflex" — the urge to fix, correct, and supply the obvious answer — is exactly what backfires. The more you push for change, the more the other person defends the status quo.

## Core principles
- **Roll with resistance.** Don't meet a defense with a counter-argument. Reflect it back, soften it, ask about it.
- **Ask open, non-judgmental questions** that evoke *self-change talk*: "What would have to be true for you to want to cut back?" "What do you like about it — and what's the not-so-good side?"
- **Express empathy.** Understanding first, agenda second. People change in relationships where they feel heard, not judged.
- **Support autonomy.** "It's your call" is not a surrender — it's what *lowers* the defensiveness that blocks change ([[Psychological Reactance]]).

## Why it works
Pushing puts the other person in the role of *defending the behavior*. MI flips it so they voice the reasons to change themselves — which they believe far more than reasons you supply. It pairs directly with [[CRAFT (Community Reinforcement and Family Training)]] as the conversation skill a family member uses.
