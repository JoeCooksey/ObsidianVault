## Top Stories

### 1. **TSMC Targets 130k CoWoS Wafers/Month by Year-End — Supply-Demand Gap Narrows to 10%**

TSMC is on track to reach 130,000 [[CoWoS]] wafers per month by late 2026, nearly quadrupling its late-2024 capacity of ~35,000 wafers/month. The expansion centers on the AP7 facility in Chiayi and the newly acquired AP8 site in Tainan, with a phased build-out continuing through 2027. TrendForce reports the CoWoS supply-demand gap will narrow from ~20% to ~10% by Q4 2026. NVIDIA currently secures over 60% of total CoWoS allocation. TSMC's 2026 CapEx stands at $52--56 billion, with advanced packaging consuming an increasing share.

> **Why it matters:** CoWoS is the packaging bottleneck that actually limits how many AI accelerators can ship. The 4x capacity ramp is the single largest constraint-relief event in the AI hardware supply chain. For power and signal integrity engineers, the move to larger interposer sizes (CoWoS-L reticle stitching) and higher layer counts introduces new PDN impedance, thermal, and warpage challenges that didn't exist at 35k wafers/month.

### 2. **TEGNet: AI Designs Thermoelectric Generators 10,000x Faster, Achieves ~9% Conversion Efficiency**

Researchers at Japan's National Institute for Materials Science published TEGNet, a composable neural-network surrogate for finite-element simulation of [[Thermoelectric Generator|thermoelectric generators]]. The system predicts device performance with >99% accuracy while cutting computational time by ~10,000x. Fabricated prototypes achieved conversion efficiencies of ~9% for segmented legs and 8.7% for n-p paired generators under near-room-temperature industrial waste-heat conditions (deltaT ~200 K). TEGNet also identified designs that avoid bismuth telluride (Bi2Te3), reducing material cost. Published in *Nature Reviews Electrical Engineering*.

> **Why it matters:** 9% conversion efficiency at industrial waste-heat temperatures is among the highest reported for bulk TEG devices. The real breakthrough is the surrogate model: evaluating thousands of leg-geometry and material-segmentation trade-offs in seconds rather than weeks of FEA. This is the kind of AI-for-EE-design workflow that will generalize to any multi-physics optimization problem — [[MEMS]] actuators, RF matching networks, transformer core geometries.

### 3. **Architect Labs Emerges from Stealth with $24M for AI-Automated Chip Design**

Palo Alto startup Architect Labs raised $24 million in seed funding (led by Kindred Ventures) and emerged from stealth on June 18. The company is building an AI platform that explores, designs, and formally verifies custom chips end-to-end. The 18-person team includes researchers from Anthropic, DeepMind, xAI, Meta, Google, and Intel with 80+ collective tape-outs. The stated goal: a "designless semiconductor industry" where organizations specify workload requirements and receive verified RTL without maintaining an internal chip-design team.

> **Why it matters:** The EDA industry has been adding AI features incrementally (Synopsys DSO.ai, Cadence Cerebrus), but Architect Labs is attempting a full-stack replacement of the human design loop — from architecture exploration through formal verification. If the "formally verified" claim holds for production-class designs (not just toy benchmarks), this changes the economics of custom silicon from a $50M+ NRE commitment to something closer to a software deployment. Watch whether their first tape-outs target inference ASICs or something more general.

### 4. **CMOS Chip Drives Parallel Enzymatic DNA Synthesis — 256 Independent Sites on Silicon**

Published in *Nature Electronics*: a [[CMOS]] integrated circuit with 256 ring-electrode pairs performs spatially programmable electrochemical pH control to drive parallel enzymatic DNA synthesis in aqueous solution. Each electrode pair functions as an independent synthesis site. The team demonstrated encoding a 169-byte text message into DNA sequences synthesized entirely on-chip. The approach eliminates the need for traditional phosphoramidite chemistry (which requires anhydrous solvents) by using terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) enzymes activated by local pH gradients generated at each electrode.

> **Why it matters:** This is a textbook example of CMOS meeting biology. The electrode array is standard 180 nm CMOS — no exotic process — and the synthesis happens in water at room temperature. For EE designers, the interesting problem is electrode-to-electrode crosstalk: maintaining independent pH gradients at ~50 um pitch requires careful guard-ring design and real-time current control. Scaling to 10,000+ sites would make on-chip DNA data storage and rapid diagnostic probe synthesis practical.

### 5. **Interferroic Magnetoelectric Coupling Demonstrated in 2D Van der Waals Heterostructure**

Published in *Nature Electronics*: University of Maryland researchers demonstrated reversible, non-volatile electrical control of magnetism by stacking exfoliated flakes of ferroelectric [[CuCrP2S6]] onto ferromagnetic [[Fe3GeTe2]]. Polarization switching in the CuCrP2S6 layer modulated the magnetic coercivity of a 3.8 nm thick Fe3GeTe2 layer by ~14 mT at 153 K, with a control efficiency of ~65%. This is the first experimental demonstration of 2D ferroelectric control of 2D magnetism in an all-van-der-Waals system.

> **Why it matters:** Magnetoelectric coupling in bulk multiferroics has been pursued for decades (BiFeO3, etc.) but always at the cost of weak coupling or high operating temperatures. Doing it in a 2D vdW heterostructure means the interface is atomically clean (no lattice mismatch strain), the layers are independently selectable, and the whole stack is a few nanometers thick. A 65% control efficiency at 153 K is not room-temperature-ready, but it establishes the proof of concept for [[Spintronics|spintronic]] memory elements where you write with voltage and read with magnetoresistance — eliminating the write-current bottleneck in STT-MRAM.

### 6. **Gallium Oxide Approaches Commercial Viability — BFOM 3,300, 6-inch Substrates in Development**

A 2026 landscape review highlights beta-Ga2O3 as the leading [[Ultra-Wide Bandgap Semiconductors|UWBG]] candidate for next-generation power electronics. Key figures: 4.9 eV bandgap, theoretical breakdown field of 8 MV/cm, and a Baliga figure of merit (BFOM) of ~3,300 — 3-10x higher than [[Silicon Carbide Power Electronics|SiC]] or [[GaN Power Devices|GaN]]. Japanese producers Novel Crystal Technology and Tamura Corporation are developing 4-inch and 6-inch native substrates using edge-defined film-fed growth (EFG) and Czochralski methods — the same melt-growth techniques used for silicon, which could yield substrate costs far below SiC. Remaining challenges: poor thermal conductivity (10-27 W/mK vs. 490 W/mK for SiC) and the absence of viable p-type doping.

> **Why it matters:** The BFOM number tells you the theoretical ceiling — Ga2O3 can handle higher voltages at lower on-resistance than anything in SiC or GaN. But the thermal conductivity gap is brutal: without heterogeneous integration onto SiC or diamond heat spreaders, high-current Ga2O3 devices will thermally limit long before they hit their electrical breakdown. The substrate cost advantage (melt growth vs. sublimation for SiC) is the economic argument that keeps Ga2O3 funded. EEs designing power converters should track this material for >3.3 kV applications where SiC starts hitting its own limits.

## 🔬 Daily Deep Dive

### TEGNet and AI-Driven Thermoelectric Generator Design

**What it is:** TEGNet is a composable neural-network emulator that acts as a fast surrogate for finite-element thermoelectric simulation, enabling rapid exploration of leg geometry, material segmentation, and contact resistance trade-offs for thermoelectric generators (TEGs).

**Why now:** The TEGNet paper (published April 2026 in *Nature Reviews Electrical Engineering*, covered by IEEE Spectrum in June) demonstrated fabricated devices hitting ~9% conversion efficiency at near-room-temperature deltaT — and the AI-driven design loop that found those configurations evaluated candidates 10,000x faster than conventional FEA. This sits at the intersection of two trends: (1) AI surrogate models replacing physics solvers, and (2) renewed commercial interest in waste-heat harvesting for data centers and EV thermal management.

**Technical bullet points:**

- **Architecture:** TEGNet is a composable neural network — individual leg-level emulators are trained on FEA data, then composed into full-generator models. This modularity means adding a new thermoelectric material requires retraining only the leg-level network, not the whole system. Input features include leg geometry (length, cross-section), material Seebeck coefficient S(T), electrical resistivity rho(T), thermal conductivity kappa(T), and contact resistance.
- **Training data and accuracy:** The network was trained on ~50,000 FEA simulations spanning Bi2Te3, PbTe, half-Heusler, and skutterudite material systems. Reported prediction accuracy: >99% correlation with FEA for output power and conversion efficiency across the training domain. Inference time: milliseconds per candidate vs. minutes per FEA run.
- **Fabricated results:** Segmented-leg devices (Bi2Te3 near cold side, half-Heusler near hot side) achieved ~9% conversion efficiency at deltaT ~ 200 K. N-p paired generators hit 8.7%. These are among the highest reported for near-room-temperature operation. The AI also found competitive designs that eliminated Bi2Te3 entirely, using only half-Heusler and skutterudite segments.
- **Optimization landscape:** TEGNet explored >100,000 design candidates in under an hour — evaluating segment lengths, cross-section ratios, number of segments, and material combinations. The Pareto front of efficiency vs. power density revealed non-obvious trade-offs: e.g., shorter legs sacrifice efficiency but increase volumetric power density by 3x, relevant for space-constrained automotive applications.
- **Limitations:** The surrogate is only as good as its training domain. Extrapolation to novel materials (e.g., SnSe, organic thermoelectrics) requires new FEA data. Contact resistance modeling assumes ideal metallization — real-world degradation under thermal cycling is not captured. No coupled structural/thermal fatigue modeling.

**Where to start:**

- IEEE Xplore: search "neural network surrogate thermoelectric generator optimization" and "TEGNet composable emulator"
- The original paper in *Nature Reviews Electrical Engineering* (April 2026) — focus on the Methods section for network architecture and the Supplementary for FEA validation
- Background: Snyder & Toberer, "Complex thermoelectric materials," *Nature Materials* 7, 105 (2008) for thermoelectric fundamentals
- Related wiki pages: [[Thermoelectric Generator]], [[Surrogate Modeling]], [[Seebeck Effect]], [[Bi2Te3]]

**Calculation to work through:** A single thermoelectric leg operates between T_hot = 473 K and T_cold = 293 K (deltaT = 180 K). The material has S = 200 uV/K, rho = 1.0e-5 Ohm-m, kappa = 1.5 W/(m-K). The leg is 5 mm long with a 1 mm^2 cross-section. Calculate (a) the maximum conversion efficiency using the exact ZT-dependent formula eta_max = (deltaT/T_hot) * (sqrt(1+ZT_avg) - 1) / (sqrt(1+ZT_avg) + T_cold/T_hot), (b) the matched-load output power, and (c) how efficiency changes if you segment the leg into two materials (top half: S=180 uV/K, kappa=2.0; bottom half: S=220 uV/K, kappa=1.2) — does segmentation help or hurt at this deltaT?
