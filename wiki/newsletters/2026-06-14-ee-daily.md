# EE Daily — June 14, 2026

*Daily briefing on electrical engineering news, breakthroughs, and one topic worth a deep dive.*

## Top Stories

**1. NVIDIA + TSMC Expand AI-in-Fab Partnership — cuLitho Hits Production Scale**

NVIDIA's [[cuLitho]] computational lithography software is now deployed across TSMC's fab operations, achieving 20–50% improvement in cost effectiveness or cycle time depending on process step. The collaboration extends to transistor simulation, defect inspection, and factory scheduling using NVIDIA accelerated computing. TSMC CEO confirmed 30%+ revenue growth for 2026 driven by AI demand, even while warning global capacity may fail to meet needs for several years.

> **Why it matters:** cuLitho is a proof-of-concept for the AI-in-fab thesis — GPU-accelerated simulation replacing months of empirical iteration. For EEs, the signal is that design-technology co-optimization (DTCO) loops are now running on the same hardware as the chips being designed. Expect this to compress process node qualification timelines by 12–18 months.

**2. AMD MI450 Custom Chip Seals Multi-Year Meta Deal — Citi Upgrades to Buy, PT $575**

Citi analyst Atif Malik upgraded AMD to Buy from Neutral and raised his price target to $575 (from $460), centering the thesis on AMD's custom MI450 GPUs, which offer Meta Platforms lower total cost of ownership than NVIDIA alternatives, backed by a six-gigawatt, four-year supply agreement. BofA simultaneously raised AMD's target to $560, citing the upcoming Venice CPU launch at AMD's Advancing AI 2026 event. Q1 revenue came in at $10.25B (+38% YoY); Q2 guidance is $11.2B midpoint (+45% YoY).

> **Why it matters:** The MI450 isn't a commodity GPU — it's a custom-configured CDNA architecture built around Meta's specific memory bandwidth and inference throughput requirements. Six gigawatts over four years is not a trial run; it's an architectural commitment. The competitive implication is that NVIDIA's moat is now more about software/CUDA than silicon uniqueness.

**3. TSMC to Raise 3nm Process Pricing 15% in H2 2026**

TSMC plans to increase pricing on its N3 (3-nanometer) process node by up to 15% in the second half of 2026, driven by accelerating adoption from NVIDIA, AMD, Google, and AWS cloud service providers. Intel CEO Lip-Bu Tan visited TSMC management ahead of Computex to discuss foundry strategy. TSMC's Arizona fab remains fully booked through 2027 with no available capacity slots.

> **Why it matters:** A 15% foundry cost increase flows directly into chip BOM — affecting everything from consumer GPUs to data center accelerators. For system designers, it signals that N3 tape-out economics are tightening; expect migration pressure toward N2 GAA for next-gen designs, and increased interest in N4/N5 for cost-sensitive products where N3 pricing no longer pencils out.

**4. SK Hynix–NVIDIA Lock HBM4 Supply Through 2030 — Speeds Hit 11 Gbps / 2.8 TB/s**

NVIDIA has secured HBM4 memory supply from SK Hynix through 2030, with the current roadmap running TSMC's 12nm node for standard HBM4 and a premium custom variant (C-HBM4E) using TSMC's 3nm node for flagship accelerators. Transfer speeds have reached 11 Gbps per pin, yielding total stacked bandwidth exceeding 2.8 terabytes per second. Micron is targeting 15,000 HBM4 wafers/month capacity by end of 2026.

> **Why it matters:** HBM4 at 2.8 TB/s is a step-change from HBM3E's 1.2 TB/s ceiling — the memory wall gets pushed back but not eliminated. The C-HBM4E variant using 3nm logic die is architecturally significant: it means the memory controller itself is running at leading-edge node, enabling smarter near-memory compute. The locked supply agreement through 2030 means Samsung and Micron must catch up without guaranteed NVIDIA revenue — a competitive disadvantage that may take two product cycles to resolve.

**5. China Advances SiC Manufacturing — 14-inch Wafer + 40 µm Laser Lift-Off**

Two parallel breakthroughs in Chinese SiC manufacturing: Tiancheng Semiconductor demonstrated a 14-inch silicon carbide single crystal (industry standard is currently 8-inch moving to 6-inch-to-8-inch transition), and a Chinese equipment supplier achieved mass production of 8-inch SiC laser lift-off technology with material loss of only 40 µm — breaking the industry's longstanding 60–80 µm barrier. HRL Laboratories (separately) achieved its 40-nanometer [[GaN-on-SiC]] process at Manufacturing Readiness Level 6 for RF-GaN.

> **Why it matters:** SiC wafer cost is dominated by the boule-to-wafer slicing process — reducing kerf loss from 80 µm to 40 µm effectively halves waste per wafer, directly cutting die cost 15–25% at scale. The 14-inch roadmap matters because wafer area scales with r², so a 14-inch wafer has 3× the area of an 8-inch; combined with laser lift-off improvements, China is building a structural cost advantage in [[SiC Power Electronics]] that Western manufacturers will struggle to close quickly.

**6. 2D Fe₂Mo₃O₈ Ferroelectric — 230 µC/cm² Polarization, 4×10⁹ Cycle Endurance**

Researchers reported synthesis of a 2D Fe₂Mo₃O₈ semiconductor single crystal via oxygen-self-supply chemical vapor deposition, achieving ultrahigh ferroelectric polarization of 230 µC/cm² and endurance of 4×10⁹ cycles — ten to one hundred times higher than most 2D ferroelectric materials. A parallel paper in *Advanced Materials* demonstrated hybrid 2D ferroelectric systems (FeFETs combining van der Waals semiconductors with ferroelectric gates) for neuromorphic computing applications.

> **Why it matters:** 230 µC/cm² is exceptional — Pb(Zr,Ti)O₃ (PZT), the workhorse of commercial ferroelectrics, sits at ~50–80 µC/cm². If this polarization is confirmed in scaled FeFET geometries, the implication is non-volatile memory with 4× the margin window of HZO-based FeFETs at equivalent channel dimensions. The neuromorphic angle (CuInP₂S₆, Fe₂Mo₃O₈ as synaptic weights) points toward analog in-memory computing at sub-10nm feature sizes. See [[2D Ferroelectric Semiconductors]] and [[Neuromorphic Computing]].

**7. QuiX Quantum FFCU — Photonic Feed-Forward Control at 150 ns**

QuiX Quantum installed its Feed-Forward Control Unit (FFCU) in June 2026, enabling real-time adaptive operations within its photonic quantum computing stack at latencies of approximately 150 ns. The FFCU closes the feedback loop required for measurement-based quantum computing — where subsequent gate operations depend on mid-circuit measurement outcomes — and is designed to interface with classical HPC networks for hybrid quantum-classical workflows.

> **Why it matters:** Measurement-based photonic QC has been theoretically preferable for a decade, but classical feed-forward at useful speeds was the missing piece. 150 ns loop latency is at the edge of viability for photonic systems (photon coherence times are nanoseconds-to-microseconds depending on the platform). This is an important engineering milestone, not a qubit count record — it's the infrastructure that makes the qubit count meaningful. See [[Silicon Photonics]] and [[Quantum Computing]].

## Daily Deep Dive: Memristors and Neuromorphic Computing

**What it is:** A memristor (memory + resistor) is a two-terminal passive circuit element whose resistance depends on the history of current flow through it. It was theoretically predicted by [[Leon Chua]] in 1971 and physically realized by HP Labs in 2008. Unlike CMOS logic, memristors perform computation directly in the storage element — enabling in-memory computing that eliminates the von Neumann bottleneck.

**Why it's timely:** Nature Reviews Electrical Engineering (June 2026) published a feature on memristor-based platforms demonstrating how environmental signals can drive computation directly, pointing toward autonomous, ultralow-power [[Neuromorphic Computing]] systems. With AI inference energy costs dominating data center PUE, the ~1 fJ/synaptic operation of memristive crossbars vs ~100 pJ for SRAM bitcell operations is a 100,000× efficiency delta worth understanding.

**Technical bullets:**
- **Device physics:** Resistance switching occurs via ion migration (filamentary, HRS/LRS) or phase change. Key figures of merit: ON/OFF ratio (>10⁴ for memory, >10² for neuromorphic), switching energy (<1 fJ for analog weight update), retention (>10 years at 85°C), endurance (>10⁹ cycles for NVM)
- **Crossbar array:** M×N grid of memristors implements matrix-vector multiply in O(1) time using Kirchhoff's current law — each column current = ΣᵢGᵢⱼVᵢ, where Gᵢⱼ is conductance (synaptic weight). No data movement, no FLOP-per-watt ceiling
- **Materials landscape:** HfO₂ (CMOS-compatible, low energy, high endurance), PCM Ge₂Sb₂Te₅ (multi-level cell, used by IBM in analog AI chips), NiO/TiO₂ (filamentary, volatile threshold), 2D MoS₂/graphene (monolayer precision for future scaling)
- **Precision challenge:** Analog weight updates suffer from device-to-device variation (σG/G ~5–15%) and drift (resistance creep over time). Current workarounds: multi-level averaging, periodic weight refresh, or hybrid digital/analog (8-bit DAC + memristor fine-tune)
- **Integration status:** IBM TrueNorth (4096 neurosynaptic cores, 256 synapses each), Intel Loihi 2 (CMOS + e-NVM weights), Stanford HAM-Net (HfO₂ 1T1R arrays at 40nm). Commercial: Mythic's analog matrix processor (AMP) targeting 25 TOPS/W vs GPU's ~0.5 TOPS/W for inference

**Where to start:**
- IEEE Xplore: `"memristive crossbar" "neuromorphic" weight update 2025 2026`
- IEEE Xplore: `"HfO2 memristor" analog inference "in-memory computing"`
- Wikilinks: [[Memristor]], [[Neuromorphic Computing]], [[In-Memory Computing]], [[Von Neumann Bottleneck]], [[Leon Chua]]
- Key paper: Strukov et al., *Nature* 453, 2008 (the HP realization)

**Concrete calculation to work through:** A 256×256 memristor crossbar at 100 ns inference latency computes 256² = 65,536 multiply-accumulates (MACs) in one cycle. At 65,536 MACs/100ns = 655 GOPS. If each device dissipates 1 fJ/operation, total power = 65,536 × 10⁻¹⁵ J / 10⁻⁷ s = 655 µW. Compare: a GPU achieving 1 TOPS at 300W — the crossbar has ~5,000× better energy efficiency at this scale. What breaks this at 1024×1024? Work through the IR drop across the interconnect.
