# EE Daily — June 20, 2026

*Daily briefing on electrical engineering news, breakthroughs, and one topic worth a deep dive.*

## Top Stories

**1. Intel 18A-P Enters Risk Production — 9% Performance Gain at Iso-Power, 40% Thermal Resistance Reduction**
Intel announced at the 2026 VLSI Symposium on June 16 that its 18A-P process node has entered risk production, hitting the timeline originally promised to customers in 2025. The 18A-P is a performance-optimized evolution of the base [[Intel 18A]] node, delivering either 9% higher performance at iso-power or 18% lower power at iso-performance, with a 40% reduction in thermal resistance through transistor, contact, interconnect, and thermal path improvements. Critically, 18A-P is a drop-in upgrade requiring no chip redesign from customers already targeting 18A — Apple and Google have reportedly expressed interest, and Intel has formed a strategic partnership with Taiwan's UMC to challenge TSMC's foundry dominance.

> **Why it matters:** Risk production means the process design kit is locked and running on production equipment — customers can commit tape-outs, though yields are not yet at HVM levels. The drop-in compatibility is the real story: it eliminates the 12-18 month redesign cycle that normally gates process adoption in the foundry business. For chip architects evaluating foundry options, 9% performance at iso-power translates to roughly one additional pipeline stage or wider execution at the same clock and thermal budget. The 40% thermal resistance cut addresses the acute junction-to-case thermal bottleneck that has plagued backside power delivery architectures. If Intel can demonstrate competitive defect densities (D0 < 0.1/cm^2) during risk production, 18A-P becomes a credible alternative to TSMC N2P for 2027 HVM tape-outs.

**2. SK Hynix Ships 12-Layer HBM4E Samples — 48 GB per Stack, 16 Gbps/pin, ~4 TB/s Bandwidth**
SK Hynix announced on June 18, 2026 that it has shipped 12-layer [[HBM4E]] samples to major customers, achieving 48 GB capacity per stack with per-pin data rates of 16 Gbps and aggregate bandwidth approaching 4 TB/s. Power efficiency improved by more than 20% over HBM4. Samsung had begun sampling its own HBM4E design on May 29 at 14 Gbps/pin, putting SK Hynix's 16 Gbps samples ahead on raw bandwidth. SK Hynix holds roughly 58% of global HBM revenue as of Q1 2026 (Counterpoint Research), and these samples target the qualification pipeline for NVIDIA's Rubin Ultra platform shipping in 2027.

> **Why it matters:** HBM4E is the seventh generation of high-bandwidth memory and the first to push per-pin rates to 16 Gbps — up from 9.6 Gbps in HBM3E. The 12-layer stack achieves 48 GB by using 32-Gbit DRAM dies, which implies aggressive TSV pitch scaling (likely sub-30 um) and hybrid bonding or advanced thermocompression at the die-to-die interfaces. For system architects, the ~4 TB/s per stack means a 6-stack GPU package (like Rubin Ultra) would deliver ~24 TB/s memory bandwidth — sufficient to keep a multi-hundred-TOPS accelerator fed without being memory-bound on large language model inference. The 20% power efficiency gain matters at rack scale: HBM power is already 30-40% of total GPU package power in current designs.

**3. imec and Ghent University Achieve First Heterogeneous Integration of Thin-Film LiNbO3 and LiTaO3 on Silicon Photonics**
Researchers at imec and Ghent University demonstrated the first heterogeneous integration of thin-film [[lithium niobate]] (LiNbO3) modulators on imec's [[silicon photonics]] platform using micro-transfer printing. In parallel, they achieved the first-ever integration of a [[lithium tantalate]] (LiTaO3) modulator on a silicon photonic integrated circuit. The LiTaO3 work was published in *Nature Photonics* in 2026, and the micro-transfer printing approach enables centimeter-scale printing of pre-fabricated thin-film devices onto active silicon photonics wafers at the back-end-of-line stage.

> **Why it matters:** Silicon photonics has excellent passive components (waveguides, couplers, filters) but lacks an efficient native modulator — silicon's weak electro-optic effect forces carrier-depletion modulators with limited bandwidth (~50 GHz) and high V_pi*L products. LiNbO3 and LiTaO3 have Pockels coefficients of ~30 and ~22 pm/V respectively, enabling modulators with V_pi*L below 2 V*cm and bandwidths exceeding 100 GHz. The micro-transfer printing approach is the breakthrough: it avoids the wafer bonding and etch-back processes that have limited prior thin-film LN integration attempts to small areas. Centimeter-scale printing means you can place multiple modulators, each pre-tested, onto a silicon PIC alongside [[germanium photodetectors]] and III-V lasers — a true heterogeneous transceiver-on-chip for 1.6T and 3.2T coherent links.

**4. QuiX Quantum Installs Feed-Forward Control Unit — 150 ns Deterministic Latency for Photonic Quantum Computing**
On June 4, 2026, Dutch startup QuiX Quantum announced the first installation of its Feed-Forward Control Unit (FFCU), a hardware module that converts single-photon detector signals into real-time control actions on photonic integrated circuits with a deterministic latency of approximately 150 nanoseconds. The FFCU uses FPGA-based processing and custom electronics to enable measurement-based quantum computation, where the outcome of one photon measurement determines subsequent gate operations on remaining photons traversing the optical circuit.

> **Why it matters:** Feed-forward is the critical missing piece for universal photonic quantum computing. In measurement-based (cluster-state) architectures, computation proceeds by measuring individual qubits and applying conditional operations to neighbors based on the measurement outcome — without feed-forward, only non-adaptive (Clifford) circuits are possible, which are classically simulable. The 150 ns latency is tight but physically consistent: photons in a ~30 m fiber delay line travel for ~150 ns, giving the FPGA exactly enough time to process the detector click and actuate a phase shifter or switch on the PIC. For comparison, superconducting qubit feed-forward operates at ~500 ns-1 us latency. The system-level integration — detectors, FPGA, PIC control — is what distinguishes this from academic feed-forward demos that used benchtop instruments.

**5. TEGNet: AI Designs Thermoelectric Generators 10,000x Faster Than Conventional Methods — Published in Nature**
Researchers led by Takao Mori at NIMS (National Institute for Materials Science, Japan) published in *Nature* (April 15, 2026) a composable neural emulator called TEGNet that designs [[thermoelectric generators]] (TEGs) at 10,000x the speed of commercial finite-element solvers while maintaining over 99% accuracy. The team fabricated optimized devices achieving conversion efficiencies exceeding 9% for segmented thermoelectric legs and 8.7% for n-p paired generators — among the highest reported for near-room-temperature (300-500 K) systems. TEGNet evaluates thousands of material/geometry configurations in seconds using only 0.01% of the compute time of conventional approaches.

> **Why it matters:** Thermoelectric generator design is a coupled thermal-electrical-mechanical optimization problem with a combinatorial design space (material segmentation, leg geometry, contact resistance, thermal grease properties). Traditional FEA solvers take hours per configuration, limiting exploration to small parameter sweeps. TEGNet's neural emulator approach — trained on FEA data, then used as a differentiable surrogate — enables gradient-based optimization over the full design space. The 9% conversion efficiency at near-room-temperature is significant: the theoretical Carnot limit for a DeltaT of 200 K at 400 K hot side is only ~33%, so 9% represents a ZT-effective of roughly 1.5-2.0, which is competitive with the best Bi2Te3-based segmented devices. For power electronics engineers designing waste-heat recovery in EV powertrains or industrial processes, AI-driven TEG design could close the gap between lab prototypes and cost-effective deployed systems.

**6. Bosch Begins SiC Chip Production at Roseville — Targeting 40% of U.S. SiC Device Manufacturing Capacity**
Bosch's $1.9 billion investment in its Roseville, California fab is reaching the production milestone in 2026, with the facility beginning to manufacture 200 mm [[silicon carbide]] power semiconductors. Backed by $225 million in CHIPS Act funding, the Roseville site is expected to produce the majority of Bosch's total SiC capacity and could comprise more than 40% of all U.S.-based SiC device manufacturing when at full capacity. Bosch announced in April 2026 that it is already sampling third-generation SiC chips to global automakers.

> **Why it matters:** The transition from 150 mm to 200 mm SiC wafers is a critical inflection point for cost reduction — 200 mm wafers yield ~1.78x more die area than 150 mm, and the amortization of fab equipment over more devices per wafer drives SiC device costs toward the $0.05-0.10/A range needed for mass-market EV adoption. Bosch's third-generation SiC MOSFETs likely target R_DS(on) * A below 2.0 mohm*cm^2 at 1200 V — competitive with Infineon's CoolSiC Gen2 and Wolfspeed's Gen4 devices. Controlling 40% of U.S. SiC capacity gives Bosch significant pricing power and supply security for its automotive OEM customers (VW, BMW, Stellantis) who are increasingly requiring dual-sourced SiC for 800V EV platforms.

## Daily Deep Dive: Heterogeneous Integration of Electro-Optic Materials on Silicon Photonics via Micro-Transfer Printing

**What it is:** Micro-transfer printing (MTP) is a deterministic pick-and-place technique that lifts pre-fabricated thin-film devices from a source wafer using an elastomeric stamp and prints them onto a target wafer — in this case, placing thin-film lithium niobate (TFLN) or lithium tantalate (TFLT) modulators onto active silicon photonics circuits. Unlike wafer bonding (which joins entire wafers and then etches back), MTP allows selective placement of individual devices or arrays, enabling heterogeneous integration of multiple material systems on a single PIC. The imec/Ghent breakthrough published in *Nature Photonics* represents the first successful integration of TFLT modulators on a silicon PIC and the first centimeter-scale MTP of TFLN. This topic connects [[silicon photonics]], [[lithium niobate photonics]], [[electro-optic modulators]], and [[heterogeneous integration]] — essential for anyone working on next-generation optical transceivers, coherent communications, or photonic AI accelerators.

**5 Technical Bullets:**

- **Why Silicon Needs Help:** Silicon's centrosymmetric crystal structure means it has no linear electro-optic (Pockels) effect. Current silicon modulators use carrier depletion in reverse-biased p-n junctions to change refractive index via the plasma dispersion effect. This gives modulation bandwidth limited by carrier transit time (~40-60 GHz), high insertion loss (4-7 dB for a Mach-Zehnder), and V_pi*L products of 1.5-2.5 V*cm. LiNbO3 (r33 = 30.9 pm/V) and LiTaO3 (r33 = 22 pm/V) offer pure electro-optic modulation with no free-carrier absorption penalty, enabling V_pi*L below 2 V*cm, bandwidths >100 GHz, and insertion loss <1 dB.

- **Micro-Transfer Printing Process:** Source wafers contain arrays of pre-fabricated TFLN or TFLT modulators on a sacrificial release layer (typically SiO2 on LiNbO3-on-insulator). An elastomeric PDMS stamp picks up devices by exploiting rate-dependent adhesion — fast retraction creates strong adhesion (pick-up), slow retraction creates weak adhesion (print). The stamp transfers devices onto the silicon PIC with sub-micron alignment accuracy using visual fiducials. Imec demonstrated centimeter-scale prints, meaning a single stamp operation can place a modulator array spanning an entire transceiver channel set.

- **Optical Coupling Challenge:** The mode profile mismatch between a silicon waveguide (effective index ~2.4, mode area ~0.1 um^2) and a TFLN ridge waveguide (effective index ~2.0, mode area ~0.5 um^2) requires adiabatic tapers or inverse tapers at the interface. The vertical offset between the printed TFLN layer and the silicon waveguide layer must be controlled to within +/- 50 nm for efficient evanescent coupling. Reported coupling losses are in the 0.5-1.5 dB per transition range — competitive with bonded approaches but with the advantage of post-fabrication integration that preserves silicon PIC yield.

- **LiTaO3 vs. LiNbO3 Trade-offs:** Lithium tantalate has a lower Pockels coefficient (r33 ~ 22 pm/V vs. 31 pm/V for LN), but it offers several advantages: (1) lower optical absorption in the visible/near-UV, enabling applications below 600 nm; (2) higher photorefractive damage threshold, important for high-power applications; (3) compatible thin-film fabrication via ion slicing from bulk crystals, similar to TFLN. The Nature Photonics paper on the heterogeneous LiTaO3 platform demonstrated modulator bandwidths exceeding 40 GHz — sufficient for 100 GBaud PAM-4 with equalization.

- **Implications for 1.6T and 3.2T Transceivers:** Next-generation pluggable optics (1.6T-DR8, 3.2T) require 200 GBaud signaling per lane. Silicon carrier-depletion modulators are running out of bandwidth headroom at 100-120 GBaud. Heterogeneously integrated TFLN modulators on silicon PICs could provide the >100 GHz analog bandwidth needed, while keeping the silicon platform's mature passive components, germanium photodetectors, and CMOS driver co-integration. The MTP approach also enables known-good-die modulator integration — each TFLN modulator can be tested on the source wafer before transfer, avoiding yield loss from defective modulators on an otherwise functional silicon PIC.

**Concrete Calculation to Work Through:**
*Estimate the half-wave voltage-length product (V_pi*L) for a TFLN Mach-Zehnder modulator and compare to silicon:*
- Pockels coefficient of LiNbO3 (r33): 30.9 pm/V
- Refractive index of LN at 1550 nm (extraordinary): n_e = 2.138
- Electro-optic index change: Delta_n = (1/2) * n_e^3 * r33 * E
- For a phase shift of pi: Delta_n * L = lambda / 2 = 775 nm
- With electrode gap g = 5 um: E = V_pi / g
- Therefore: (1/2) * n_e^3 * r33 * (V_pi / g) * L = 775 nm
- V_pi * L = (2 * 775 nm * g) / (n_e^3 * r33) = (2 * 775e-9 * 5e-6) / (2.138^3 * 30.9e-12)
- V_pi * L = 7.75e-12 / (9.756 * 30.9e-12) = 7.75e-12 / 3.015e-10 = **0.026 V*m = 2.6 V*cm**
- Real devices achieve 1.5-2.0 V*cm with optimized overlap factors (Gamma ~ 0.6-0.8). Compare to silicon carrier-depletion: V_pi*L ~ 1.5-2.5 V*cm but with 4-7 dB excess loss and bandwidth limited to ~50 GHz.
