# Research: Artificial Sweeteners — Studies & Merit in Diet Sodas

## Overview
The artificial-sweetener literature is **large, active, and genuinely split**: short-term randomized trials find sweeteners help (or don't hurt) weight and glucose control versus sugar, while long-term observational cohorts associate them with more diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and mortality. The contradiction is mostly explained by **[[Reverse Causation in Sweetener Cohort Studies|reverse causation]]** — sick and dieting people choose diet drinks. The defensible bottom line: **the sweeteners in diet sodas carry real merit as a sugar-replacement / harm-reduction tool, modest merit as a weight-loss aid, and are not the health hazard the headlines suggest — but they are not as good as water.**

> [!answer] How much merit? See the one-page scorecard: [[Non-Nutritive Sweeteners and Diet Soda Merit Verdict]].

## Key Findings

### Weight & metabolism
- **RCTs**: replacing sugar with sweeteners produces modest weight loss or neutral effects. A 52-week RCT found NNS beverages gave *slightly more* weight loss than water (7.5 vs 6.1 kg) — not clinically significant (Source: [[Harrold 2023 - NNS Beverages vs Water RCT]]).
- **Meta-analysis**: pooled RCTs show **no significant BMI effect**; pooled cohorts link sweeteners to *higher* BMI and cardiometabolic risk, with publication bias for diabetes (Source: [[Meta-Analyses of Nonsugar Sweeteners (Azad 2017 + Toews 2019)]]).
- **WHO 2023** conditionally recommends **against** using sweeteners for weight control — no long-term fat-loss benefit, possible long-term harm signals (Source: [[WHO 2023 Guideline on Non-Sugar Sweeteners]]).

### Cardiovascular
- **NutriNet-Santé** (n=103,388): higher intake → +9% overall CVD risk (HR 1.09); aspartame → stroke, ace-K/sucralose → coronary disease — but small absolute risk (Source: [[Debras 2022 - Artificial Sweeteners and Cardiovascular Disease (NutriNet-Sante)]]).
- **UK Biobank** (n=133,285): tiny per-unit CVD hazard, and **~70% mediated by pre-existing type 2 diabetes** — a strong reverse-causation fingerprint (Source: [[Sun 2024 - Artificial Sweeteners and CVD (UK Biobank)]]).
- **Erythritol** (a sugar alcohol, *not* in most diet sodas): linked to ~2× cardiac-event risk and promotes clotting — relevant to keto/blend products, not standard diet soda (Source: [[Witkowski 2023 - Erythritol and Cardiovascular Risk (Nature Medicine)]]).

### Cancer (aspartame)
- **IARC 2023**: aspartame **Group 2B "possibly carcinogenic"** — limited evidence. **JECFA** reaffirmed the ADI (40 mg/kg = ~9–14 cans/day) and found no safety concern. Hazard ≠ risk (Source: [[IARC-JECFA 2023 Aspartame Assessment]], [[Aspartame]]).

### Gut microbiome & glucose
- **Human RCT** (n=120): saccharin and sucralose impaired glucose tolerance at below-ADI doses; all four sweeteners shifted the microbiome; effects are **person-specific** and microbiome-transmissible (Source: [[Suez 2022 - Sweeteners Alter Human Glucose Tolerance (Cell)]], [[Personalized Glycemic Response to Sweeteners]]).
- Sucralose/saccharin reduce butyrate-producing bacteria; aspartame's signal is weaker (Source: [[Artificial Sweeteners Gut Review]]).

- [[RCT vs Cohort Evidence Split (Nonsugar Sweeteners)]]: the two best designs disagree; know which one a headline uses.
- [[Reverse Causation in Sweetener Cohort Studies]]: why "diet soda → disease" cohort claims are hypothesis-generating, not proof.
- [[Personalized Glycemic Response to Sweeteners]]: your microbiome decides whether a sweetener affects your blood sugar.
- [[Non-Nutritive Sweeteners Overview]]: the molecule matters — aspartame/stevia vs sucralose/saccharin vs erythritol.

## Key Entities
- [[Aspartame]]: the classic diet-soda sweetener; IARC 2B but reassuring on dose.

## Contradictions
- **RCTs vs cohorts** — the core tension, resolved mostly by confounding (see concept pages).
- **IARC vs JECFA/FDA** on aspartame — IARC flags a hazard on limited evidence; JECFA and FDA judge real-world risk negligible. Both can be "right" because they answer different questions (can it? vs does it, at these doses?).
- **WHO guideline vs its own data** — critics (*Eur J Clin Nutr* 2023) argue WHO leaned on confounded cohorts over cleaner RCTs to reach a cautionary recommendation.

## Open Questions
- No long-term RCT with hard clinical endpoints exists — the decisive study is still missing.
- Are the cohort harm signals *any* residual causal effect, or fully confounding? Unresolved.
- Which individuals are "high responders" (microbiome-driven), and can it be predicted/tested cheaply?
- Long-term safety of erythritol/xylitol specifically (JECFA review pending).
