# Research - Best Things To Do During Downtime at a Low-Demand Job

## Overview

The question — *what to do during a job that needs only ~10 min/hour of customer talk, on the phone, without permanent deep focus* — has a single governing constraint: **the hourly call is a scheduled interruption.** Once you see that, the answer isn't a list of activities, it's a *filter*: do work that interruption can't damage (low [[Interruption Resumption Cost|resumption cost]]), and bank deep work for uninterrupted time. The runnable output is **[[Low-Demand Job Downtime Playbook]]**.

## Key Findings

- **The interruption — not the boredom — is the real constraint.** Returning to an interrupted task costs a real **resumption lag** (Gloria Mark's ~23-min average); interrupted people work faster but with more stress (Source: [[Gloria Mark - The Cost of Interrupted Work]]).
- **Resumption cost is asymmetric across tasks.** Deep/creative/single-thread work holds a large mental model and gets *shredded* by interruption; atomic chunks, passive input, and routine admin hold little and survive fine → [[Fragmentation-Tolerant Activities]].
- **So the selection rule is "fragmentation-tolerant first."** Fill call gaps with chunk-sized, drop-anywhere work; **don't** start a proof, debugging, or an edge-of-ability practice rep between calls — save those for continuous off-clock blocks (Source: [[Gloria Mark - The Cost of Interrupted Work]]).
- **Conventional slow-job advice already matches the science.** Listicles for "slow days at work" recommend exactly the fragmentation-tolerant set — podcasts/audiobooks, language apps, admin, networking, light movement — and never recommend starting deep work between calls (Source: [[Indeed - Productive Things To Do During Slow Days at Work]]).
- **The dead time is a near-perfect host for a daily-learning habit.** [[Spaced Repetition]] flashcards and [[Microlearning]] *are* fragmentation-tolerant by construction, so ~50 min/hour of gaps can compound into a real skill — *if* you add a retrieval beat to passive listening (Source: learning-science cluster).
- **Passive ≠ best.** Audiobooks/podcasts tolerate interruption *because* they demand little, which also makes them low-density ([[Active vs Passive Leisure]]). The sweet spot is **chunk-sized AND effortful**: a flashcard you must retrieve, a problem you must solve.
- **Engineer the default, don't white-knuckle it.** Same lesson as the existing idle-time cluster: pre-load the good option, add friction to the feed, pick a default per energy level ([[Killing Time Substitution Playbook]], [[Phone-Reflex in Idle Moments]]).

## Key Concepts

- [[Interruption Resumption Cost]] — the tax on returning to interrupted work; the governing constraint.
- [[Fragmentation-Tolerant Activities]] — the selection filter (low resumption cost + medium holds state + self-contained chunks).
- [[Low-Demand Job Downtime Playbook]] — **the deliverable**: Tier S (effortful + interruptible), A (passive), B (admin), C (recovery), and what *not* to do.

## Contradictions

- None substantive. The behavioral-science source and the practitioner listicles point the same way; the only tension is that practitioner lists treat all options as interchangeable, whereas the science ranks them by interruption-tolerance — resolved in the playbook.

## Open Questions

- **Side-income / "phone business" angle** not deeply researched — whether interruptible micro-work (freelance micro-tasks, content batching) is worth it during gaps, vs. pure skill-building. Likely fails the focus test for anything beyond trivial tasks.
- **Employer policy** — whether personal-phone use during gaps is permitted varies by workplace; the playbook assumes it's allowed.
- **Diminishing returns on passive listening** — no clean dose data on how much audiobook/podcast intake converts to retained knowledge without active retrieval; flagged as the main failure mode.
