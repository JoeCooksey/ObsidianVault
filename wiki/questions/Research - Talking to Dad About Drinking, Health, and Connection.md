# Research: Talking to Dad About Drinking, Health, and Connection

## The situation
Joe's dad drinks most weekends. Joe (on a health kick) keeps telling him to stop and eat healthy; Dad doesn't apply it, and conversations get more awkward. Dad's interests are narrow (sports, CEOs, stock market), making shared ground hard. Dad complains Joe and Mom "gang up" on him.

## Overview
The research is unusually unanimous and it points the opposite way from intuition: **the telling-him-to-stop strategy is the thing keeping him stuck.** Across addiction science, persuasion psychology, and family-systems theory, direct pressure from people who are "obviously right" produces *more* defensiveness, not change. The leverage isn't in better arguments — it's in **changing how you relate to him**: drop the campaign, kill the tag-team, reward the good, and connect on *his* turf first.

> [!key] You're not wrong about the drinking. You're being **right in a way that doesn't work.** Correct and effective are two different problems, and only the second one helps your dad.

## Key Findings

### 1. Telling him to stop is *why* he won't — psychological reactance
When you tell someone to stop a behavior, you threaten their autonomy, so they defend the behavior harder to reclaim their freedom — independent of whether you're correct. (Source: [[Psychological Reactance]].) Nagging specifically: creates resentment, gets tuned out ("the more you nag, the less they hear"), feels controlling, and fixates on what he's *not* doing. (Source: [[Empowering Parents — 7 Reasons Why Nagging Doesnt Work]].) **Every health lecture is making him slightly more attached to the weekend drink.**

### 2. The evidence-based alternative is CRAFT — and it's dramatically better
For a loved one who hasn't asked for help and resists it, **Community Reinforcement and Family Training** beats confrontation and detachment in head-to-head RCTs: **~64–67% engagement vs ~30%** (Source: [[CRAFT — Advaita Integrated Medicine]], [[Robert J. Meyers — CRAFT Evidence]]). The method: **reward sober time** with warmth and attention, **calmly withdraw** (no fight) during drinking, **stop shielding him** from natural consequences, and **never nag/plead/threaten.** You change *your* behavior so being sober and being close to you out-rewards the drink.

### 3. Stop convincing — let him argue for change (motivational interviewing)
People believe reasons *they* voice far more than reasons you supply. The "righting reflex" (rushing in with the obvious fix) is what backfires. Ask open questions — *"What do you like about it, and what's the not-so-good side?"* — and **support his autonomy** out loud: "It's your call." (Source: [[Motivational Interviewing — Alcohol.org]].)

### 4. The "ganging up" complaint is structurally real — and it's defeating you
In family-systems terms, you and Mom form a **cross-generational coalition** against Dad. Even when the two of you are right, the *structure* guarantees he hears it as a verdict, not advice — so he tunes out and digs in. (Source: [[Triangulation — Family Systems Theory (GoodTherapy)]].) The trigger phrase is **"Mom and I both think…"**. Fix: **de-triangle** — talk to him **one-on-one, as yourself**, never as a tag-team, and drop the "we."

### 5. You can't change him — change your response (differentiation)
The convergent advice across every source: **stop spending energy trying to change your dad; redirect it into how you respond to who he actually is.** (Source: [[Differentiation, Not Estrangement (Calling Home)]].) This is [[Differentiation of Self]] — stay connected *and* separate, neither fusing (his drinking = your daily mood) nor cutting off (going cold). It's the family-systems twin of the Stoic [[Dichotomy of Control]] and [[Mel Robbins]]'s [[Let Them]] / [[Let Me]].

### 6. Connect on his turf before expecting depth
You don't need shared interests you both already love — you need a **low-stakes shared channel.** Enter *his* world first: watch a game with him, ask him to actually teach you how he reads a stock or what he thinks of a CEO. Small, consistent, repeated contact (even near-silent shared time) builds the connection; the meaningful talk comes later, through the open channel — not before it. (Source: [[Connecting With an Emotionally Distant Father (Good Men Project)]].)

### 7. Take care of yourself regardless of outcome
CRAFT families get *less* anxious, depressed, and angry **even when the loved one never changes** — because they reclaim their own life. (Source: [[CRAFT — Advaita Integrated Medicine]].) Your health obsession is good; aim it at *yourself*, model it quietly, and let it be attractive rather than assigned.

## What this looks like in practice
- **Stop**: "You drink too much," "you should eat healthy," "this is pointless," and anything starting with "Mom and I."
- **Start**:
  - Talk to him **alone**, never with Mom present, when he's **sober and relaxed**.
  - **Enter his interests** — "Teach me how you'd value this stock," "want to watch the game Saturday?" Make the *connection* the goal, not the *correction*.
  - When you do raise health/drinking, make it **brief, positive, autonomy-respecting, and "I"-framed**: *"I worry about you because I want you around a long time — but it's your call, and I'm here if you ever want to do something active together."*
  - **Reward the good**: notice and warm up when he's sober, active, or open. Quietly disengage (no lecture) when he's drinking.
  - **Model, don't preach** your own health.
- **Accept**: he may not change. Your job is your response, the relationship, and your own life — not his choices.

## Contradictions
- **"Confront him / stage an intervention" vs CRAFT.** RCT evidence is clear: confrontation underperforms the calm, reinforcement-based approach by roughly 2× on engagement. CRAFT wins. (Source: [[Robert J. Meyers — CRAFT Evidence]].)
- **"Just detach / Al-Anon" vs active engagement.** Detachment protects you but engages the drinker less than CRAFT does. The best path is *engaged but non-confrontational*, not withdrawn.

## Open Questions
- **Is this clinical-level alcohol use disorder or heavy-but-functional weekend drinking?** The strategy is similar but the stakes and urgency differ; worth honestly assessing (frequency, quantity, consequences, whether he can stop). Not determinable from the prompt.
- **Joe's own health framing.** Distinguishing genuine concern from a "health obsession" that's become its own lens is worth a separate look — pushing optimization onto others is a known failure mode.
- **Mom's role.** Real change in the coalition needs Mom to stop the tag-team too; that's a separate conversation.
