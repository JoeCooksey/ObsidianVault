# Research: How to Learn Something Every Day (The Daily Learning Habit)

## Overview

The best way to learn *daily* is not a technique — it's a **habit that carries the techniques**. The vault already documents *how to learn* (retrieval, spacing, Feynman, deliberate practice → [[Research: How to Learn Anything (The Science of Learning)]]). This research answers the missing question: **what makes that happen every single day?** The answer is a small, anchored, lapse-tolerant routine. Distribution beats volume, and consistency beats intensity.

## Key Findings

- **Distribution beats volume — this is the whole game.** Spaced study beat massed in **259 of 271 comparisons**, and even a 1-day gap beats a single session (Source: [[Cepeda 2006 - Distributed Practice Meta-Analysis]]). The same hours spread across daily sessions retain dramatically more than the same hours crammed. This is *why* "a little every day" wins — not motivation, mechanism. **(high confidence)**
- **Short sessions are enough.** Repeated 15–20 minute modules produced significant skill gains in 358 students (Source: [[Frontiers 2025 - Microlearning and Soft Skills]]). The small format is what makes a *daily* cadence physically survivable → [[Microlearning]]. **(medium confidence — self-report, quasi-experimental)**
- **The cue is the hard part; stacking solves it.** New habits fail because nothing reliably triggers them. Anchoring the learning block to an existing daily habit ("After X, I learn for 15 min") borrows an automatic trigger and satisfies *make it obvious* for free → [[Habit Stacking]]. **(high confidence)**
- **Expect ~66 days, and forgive lapses.** A daily behavior takes a median **66 days** (range 18–254) to feel automatic, faster when small; the "21 days" claim is a myth (Source: [[Lally 2009 - How Habits Are Formed (66 Days)]]). Critically, **missing one day barely dents the curve** — the killer is the all-or-nothing spiral, which [[Never Miss Twice]] defuses. **(high confidence)**
- **Reflection converts time into learning.** "Reflection turns experience into knowledge; experience alone does not" (Source: [[NIU - Reflective Journals and Learning Logs]]). A three-line daily [[Learning Log (Daily Reflection)|learning log]] (learned / fuzzy / connects-to) drives metacognition and feeds tomorrow's target. **(medium confidence)**
- **The format must contain a difficulty.** Daily *consumption* (videos, articles) builds familiarity, not skill. The session must hold a [[Desirable Difficulties|desirable difficulty]] — retrieval, a problem, a teach-back — or the streak measures nothing. **(high confidence, from existing cluster)**

> **Make it small → anchor it → make the session real → close with reflection → protect the chain.**

## Contradictions / Tensions

- **"21 days" vs "66 days":** the popular 21-day figure (Maltz 1960, [[21-Day Habit Formation System|Huberman's framing]]) is best read as *"a window to attempt deliberate change,"* not a deadline for automaticity. [[Lally 2009 - How Habits Are Formed (66 Days)|Lally's ~66-day median]] is the empirical number. Both can be true: 21 days to *start* showing change, ~66 to *automate*.
- **Microlearning hype vs evidence:** vendor sources cite huge numbers (≈150% retention gains); peer-reviewed data is real but more modest and discipline-specific. The *format's* value rests on the rock-solid spacing effect, not the marketing stats.

## Open Questions

- Optimal *time of day* for the block (interacts with [[Three-Phase Day Framework]] / circadian alertness) — not resolved here.
- How daily micro-review interacts with formal SRS scheduling (Anki intervals) when both run — likely complementary, unverified.
- Long-run adherence: most evidence is ≤12 weeks; what sustains a daily learning habit over *years* is under-studied (relationships, identity, and curiosity are the candidate drivers).
