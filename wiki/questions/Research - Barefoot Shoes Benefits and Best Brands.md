# Research: Barefoot Shoes — Benefits and Best Brands

## Overview

Barefoot (minimalist) shoes let the foot move naturally — zero drop, wide toe box, thin flexible sole — instead of cushioning and supporting it. The best-evidenced benefit is **stronger feet and lower legs** (two RCTs). Other claimed benefits (balance, lower impact, injury prevention) range from plausible to unproven, and the headline caution is real: **a too-fast transition causes injury.** On brands, the consensus tiering is Vivobarefoot (premium), Xero (value), Lems (beginner gateway), Merrell Glove (trail), and Vibram FiveFingers (max ground feel, experts only).

## Key Findings — Benefits

1. **Stronger feet — strongest evidence.** Walking in minimalist shoes increased foot muscle size and strength as much as a dedicated foot-exercise program (RCT, n=57). (Source: [[Ridge 2019 - Minimalist Walking Strengthens Foot Muscles]]) A 6-month transition increased foot **and** leg muscle volume on MRI vs control, concentrated in the forefoot. (Source: [[Chen 2016 - Minimalist Shoes Foot Muscle Volume]]) The popular "~60% foot-strength gain in 6 months" figure comes from this BYU research line. (confidence: high)
2. **Better ground feel / proprioception.** Thin soles restore sensory contact with the ground, the plausible basis for improved balance and body awareness. (confidence: medium — mechanism strong, outcome trials thin)
3. **Lower impact & forefoot strike — *with* gait retraining.** Minimalist shoes shift many runners to forefoot strike and lower loading rate, but the effect is modest unless paired with deliberate gait retraining (which converted 78% of rearfoot strikers). (Source: [[Yang 2020 - Gait Retraining Minimalist Shoes]]) (confidence: medium)
4. **Improved running economy.** Transitioned runners showed lower energy cost and faster 5 km times. (Source: [[Knapik 2021 - Update on Minimalist Running Shoes]]) (confidence: medium)
5. **Toe splay & natural foot shape.** The wide toe box lets toes spread, countering the narrowing forced by conventional shoes. (confidence: medium)
6. **Foot-strengthening may help *prevent* problems like plantar fasciitis** — but as prevention, not treatment of an active flare. (confidence: low–medium)

## Key Findings — Cautions

- **Transition slowly or get hurt.** The #1 mistake is switching too fast. Start at 30–60 min/day, add ~15–30 min every few days, full transition over 2–3 months. Soreness = adaptation; pain = back off. See [[Barefoot Shoe Transition Protocol]]. (confidence: high)
- **Injury *reduction* is not established.** Evidence is conflicting; one study even showed numerically (not significantly) more injuries in minimalist shoes, and running-related pain rose with mileage. (Source: [[Knapik 2021 - Update on Minimalist Running Shoes]])
- **Risk rises with body weight**, and for running most people should cap minimalist shoes at ~35% of training time. (Source: [[Knapik 2021 - Update on Minimalist Running Shoes]])
- **Active plantar fasciitis:** walking barefoot on hard surfaces can worsen it.

## Key Concepts
- [[Barefoot (Minimalist) Footwear]]: zero drop + wide toe box + thin flexible sole; objective spec ≤200 g, ≤20 mm stack, ≤7 mm drop.
- [[Foot Muscle Strengthening via Minimalist Footwear]]: the best-supported benefit, from two RCTs.
- [[Barefoot Shoe Transition Protocol]]: gradual 2–3 month ramp; the make-or-break factor.

## Contradictions
- **Do barefoot shoes reduce injury?** Marketing and the strengthening logic say yes; the running-injury trials say *not proven*, with some signals of more pain/injury during transition. (Source: [[Knapik 2021 - Update on Minimalist Running Shoes]]) The honest synthesis: they reliably strengthen feet; whether that nets out to fewer injuries depends heavily on transition discipline, body weight, and mileage.
- **"Zero drop" vs "barefoot":** brands like Altra are zero drop but heavily cushioned, so they do *not* deliver the ground-feel/proprioception benefit despite sharing one spec.

## Open Questions
- Does barefoot footwear reduce injury **rates** in non-runners (everyday walkers, older adults)? Little direct trial data.
- How large is the **balance/fall-prevention** benefit in older adults specifically? Mechanistically promising, under-tested.
- Long-term (multi-year) outcomes of full-time barefoot-shoe use are not well characterized.
